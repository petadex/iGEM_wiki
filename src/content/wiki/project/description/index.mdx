Nature has evolved enzymes for nearly every environment on Earth, and our long-term goal is to create a workflow that can utilize the breadth of public sequencing data to find or design a plastic-degrading enzyme *for any condition and any plastic type*. This year, we built this workflow and set our **North Star Goal** as designing an enzyme that breaks down **PET** at **room temperature**.

We have **open-sourced** everything we have built, from the PETadex dataset to the regulatory roadmap guiding the project's path toward commercialization. Our vision for PetaBite is to accelerate this field and inspire the next generation of scientists, founders, or iGEM teams to join this cause and collectively make a dent in the plastic pollution crisis.

## 1. The Problem

Plastic is the most successful material of the last century, and this material paved the way for the development of computers, cell phones, and most of the lifesaving advances of modern medicine. In 2019 alone, the world made about **460 million tonnes of it**. However, an immense amount of plastic was discarded, and of the 353 million tonnes that became waste that year, 9% was recycled, 19% was incinerated, and the rest went to landfill or into the environment [@oecdGlobalPlasticsOutlook2022]. Since mass production began, people have made roughly 8.3 billion tonnes of plastic and recycled less than a tenth of it [@geyerProductionUseFate2017].

**Unfortunately, plastic does not disappear**; rather, it fragments into microplastics under 5 mm that are now found in rivers, soil, drinking water and, in recent research, human blood [@leslieDiscoveryQuantificationPlastic2022].

**To date, most solutions have focused on managing plastic waste rather than eliminating it.** Mechanical recycling melts PET into new products, but the material degrades in quality with every cycle. Chemical recycling can recover PET's building blocks, yet virgin plastic produced from fossil fuels remains cheaper, preventing these technologies from scaling economically. Incineration simply transforms solid waste into atmospheric emissions without addressing the root problem. Most importantly, none of these approaches remove or destroy the plastic that has already entered our waterways.

That's why we're focused on wastewater. Every wastewater treatment plant acts as a collection point for a city's microplastic pollution, concentrating these particles in sludge and effluent streams. Yet despite capturing vast quantities of plastic, treatment plants lack any process capable of actually degrading it.

That bottleneck is the opportunity we're targeting. By integrating plastic-degrading enzymes directly into wastewater treatment infrastructure, we aim to transform treatment plants from passive collection systems into active microplastic degradation systems.

**Proteins** are chains of amino acids that fold into complex three-dimensional shapes. An **enzyme** is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction, and its structure has specialized pockets called active sites. These active sites bind specific molecules and position them so that particular chemical bonds are easier to break or form. After the reaction occurs, the enzyme releases the product and can repeat the process millions of times, allowing living organisms to carry out complex chemistry efficiently at the temperatures at which they naturally live.

</SideFigure>



**Two key facts to note:** an enzyme’s shape, and therefore its function, is determined by its amino acid sequence. This means that a sequence database like the PETadex can be transformed into a functional database, allowing us to predict which enzymes are likely to degrade PET and prioritize the most promising candidates for experimental testing. Fortunately, nature has spent billions of years evolving proteins for virtually every environment on Earth. As a result, for many chemical reactions, **a version of the enzyme already exists somewhere** that is adapted to the conditions we care about, whether that's cold temperatures, high salinity, extreme acidity, or other challenging environments. The challenge is finding those enzymes among nature's vast diversity.

PET, the plastic used in most water bottles, is built from two small molecules, terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG), linked together by ester bonds. Because ester bonds are common in nature, many bacteria and fungi already produce enzymes capable of breaking them apart. In 2016, researchers discovered the bacterium *Ideonella sakaiensis* growing on discarded PET near a recycling facility [@yoshidaBacteriumDegradesAssimilates2016]. It uses two enzymes in sequence. PETase cuts the polymer, releasing BHET and MHET, and any BHET it releases is cut again to MHET [@puspitasariClassIHydrophobins2021]. MHETase then converts MHET into TPA and EG. Both enzymes are needed, because PETase alone leaves the intermediate largely uncut [@yoshidaBacteriumDegradesAssimilates2016]. These products are the same monomers used to manufacture new PET, meaning complete enzymatic depolymerization can **enable true circular recycling**. At the molecular level, PETase relies on a catalytic triad of three amino acids, serine, histidine, and aspartate, and many of today's most advanced PET-degrading enzymes, including FAST-PETase, HotPETase, and LCC-ICCG, are engineered descendants of PETase or closely related cutinases.

</SideFigure>



Two key characteristics of plastics define the scope of this project. First, we focus on polyethylene terephthalate (PET) rather than plastics such as polyethylene (PE) or polypropylene (PP). Unlike PE and PP, PET contains ester bonds that many enzymes can naturally recognize and break down [@mussakhmetov2025esterases]. Decades of research have shown that enzymes such as PETases and cutinases can depolymerize PET, making biological recycling a realistic possibility [@ahituv2025diversity]. In contrast, polyolefins like PE and PP are built from highly stable carbon-carbon backbones that are largely inaccessible to known enzymes, and their low chemical reactivity, hydrophobicity, crystallinity, and limited chain flexibility make biodegradation extremely difficult [@han2024enzymatic].

Second, PET is a semicrystalline material, meaning it contains both amorphous regions, where polymer chains are loose enough for enzymes to access ester bonds, and crystalline regions, where chains are packed too tightly for efficient attack [@graefe2026enzymatic]. Near its glass transition temperature of approximately 70°C, the amorphous regions become more mobile, exposing many more bonds and accelerating degradation, which is why most enzymatic PET recycling studies operate at elevated temperatures [@brott2022engineering]. In contrast, an enzyme designed to depolymerize PET at room temperature must function on a rigid, largely inaccessible surface, making low-temperature degradation a far greater challenge and a key focus of our mission.

## 2. Why the current enzymes fail

If we already have enzymes that can break down PET, why haven't they been deployed at scale? The short answer is that the best enzymes we have today were designed for conditions that are difficult and expensive to reproduce within real-world infrastructure.

Over the past decade, the field has seen a series of breakthroughs. In 2016, Yoshida and colleagues discovered *Ideonella sakaiensis*, a bacterium capable of using PET as its sole carbon source, and isolated the enzyme IsPETase [@yoshidaBacteriumDegradesAssimilates2016]. Since then, researchers have engineered increasingly powerful variants. Tournier and colleagues developed LCC-ICCG, which depolymerized 90% of pretreated PET in just ten hours at 72 °C and established the industrial benchmark [@tournierEngineeredPETDepolymerase2020]. Lu and colleagues used a structure-based neural network to produce another industrial benchmark, FAST-PETase, in 2022, which almost completely degraded untreated post-consumer PET from 51 different thermoformed products within one week at 50 °C [@luMachineLearningaidedEngineering2022; @qu2026rational], and Bell and colleagues evolved HotPETase to work at the PET glass transition during the same year [@bellDirectedEvolutionEfficient2022]. The amount of protein engineering behind each of these advances was immense, and together they transformed enzymatic plastic recycling from a scientific curiosity into a viable technology.

Yet none of these enzymes fully solve the main problem our project aims to target: deployment. First, they were optimized to work near PET's glass transition temperature, around 70 °C, where the polymer becomes more flexible and easier to degrade. Wastewater treatment plants, however, operate at the temperature of the water that arrives, which tracks local ambient conditions and typically sits somewhere between 6 and 27 °C [@revollar2025adapting]. Heating millions of litres per day to 50 to 70 °C is an operating cost no plant would take on. Second, laboratory benchmarks are typically measured on clean PET films or pretreated powders. Real-world plastic pollution is far messier, consisting of partially crystalline particles mixed with other materials and often coated in biofilms, sludge, or organic matter. Finally, the field has been constrained by a surprisingly small search space. PAZy, the primary curated database of experimentally validated plastic-degrading enzymes, contains only 495 enzymes [@buchholzPlasticsDegradationHydrolytic2022]. As a result, a decade of protein engineering has largely focused on refining a narrow set of natural starting points rather than exploring the vast diversity of enzymes that may already exist in nature.

  1. **Further directed evolution of FAST-PETase.** FAST-PETase was not evolved

  2. **Rational design of the same PETase scaffold.** The active site of

  3. **Engineering a thermophilic chassis to provide heat.** Creating a
  biological system capable of maintaining a localized environment near 70 °C
  for PETase activity would present an immense engineering challenge and is
  likely impractical with current technologies.

Each of these approaches focuses on improving the enzymes we already know. In fact, they were among the first strategies we considered. However, they all shared the same underlying assumption: that the best starting point was an existing, well-studied PETase. Instead, we stepped back and asked a more fundamental question:

**What if nature has already evolved a better enzyme that we simply haven't discovered yet?**

## 3. Key research: Logan and the evidence that better enzymes already exist

Published in 2024, Logan is an assembly of the entire NCBI Sequence Read Archive, 27.3 million public sequencing datasets and roughly 5 × 10¹⁶ base pairs, assembled into contigs by Chikhi, Babaian and collaborators and released with a free k-mer search index [@chikhiLoganPlanetaryscaleGenome2024]. Some of our team members are on the Logan paper and were heavily involved in building the pilot PETase work that went into the paper. That pilot PETase search recovered millions of plastic-degrading enzymes, and the authors picked 161 natural homologs at random, expressed them in yeast, and screened them on BHET, the soluble PET subunit, in a 384-colony halo assay. 35 were active, and HPLC on the top hits showed two released more terephthalic acid than FAST-PETase, by up to four-fold [@chikhiLoganPlanetaryscaleGenome2024].

This pilot inspired the entire project and gave us the conviction that better enzymes exist out in nature. This is the premise underpinning our work: the question is no longer whether a better enzyme exists, but how to find that needle in this enormous haystack.


## 4. Hypothesis and Specific Goal

**Hypothesis:** Given the breadth of public sequencing datasets, highly efficient enzymes for every condition of plastic degradation exist in nature or can be leveraged to create the desired enzymes.

**Goal:** Using the PETadex, find or design a plastic-degrading enzyme that is more efficient at depolymerizing PET than FAST-PETase at room temperature.

We homed in on one use case this year rather than "any condition" to have a central goal that we can build out this workflow around. If we can achieve this milestone of finding a better room-temperature PETase, then the entire dataset, model, assay and the bioreactor would have done their job. Every other condition would just be a matter of running this workflow targeted at a different condition.

## 5. Our approach: one loop, three engines, three thrusters

Our approach is fundamentally different from most of the field. Traditionally, researchers start with a single enzyme scaffold and repeatedly engineer it for better performance. We reverse that process. Instead of beginning with optimization, we begin with discovery, searching across hundreds of millions of natural protein sequences to find entirely new starting points before engineering them further. To make this possible, we built an iterative design-build-test-learn pipeline powered by three interconnected systems. PETadex provides the sequence data, Dry Lab uses machine learning to rank the most promising candidates, Wet Lab experimentally tests those candidates, and Hardware enables high-throughput evaluation under controlled conditions. The resulting activity data is then fed back into the model, continuously improving the next round of predictions. Figure 1 illustrates this workflow.

A faster model or a more efficient screening platform can accelerate this cycle, but neither determines which problems are worth solving. That direction came from the teams working beyond the bench. Through stakeholder engagement, commercialization research, and public outreach, our Human Practices, Entrepreneurship, and Outreach teams continuously challenged our assumptions and informed key technical decisions.


### Dry Lab

The Logan paper published PETadex-Logan, which was their pilot search for plastic-degrading enzymes. Through this iGEM cycle, we performed a complete methodological and quantitative rebuild of our previous dataset and built PETadex v1.1. With this, we have created a published, public resource and trained models on top of it. This corpus holds 307,155,746 putative plastic-degrading enzyme ORFs: 302.4 million from the Logan index, 4.7 million from NCBI nr and 211 from PAZy [@petadexCorpusV112026]. They were recovered with 45 profile HMMs built from 57 PAZy reference enzymes and span eleven domain families, so the catalogue covers PET, polyurethane, polyamide, PHA and PLA chemistries, not PET alone. Hierarchical clustering at 90, 60 and 30% identity gives 18.2 million, 1.81 million and 22,235 families. HMM curation this year added roughly 8 million sequences and new domains to the earlier screen. Each 90% centroid carries SignalP 6 calls, predicted biochemical properties, taxonomy, predicted structures (with AlphaFold and ESMFold2) and an environmental profile propagated from BioSample metadata. Wastewater or sludge is the dominant environment for 740,110 clusters, giving us a direct link to our use case. Finally, per-sequence catalytic-site verification exists for 6.6 million ORFs.

On top of the corpus we folded 1.79 million 60% centroids with ESMFold2 (852 GPU-hours), assembled 13,092 published activity measurements on 1,219 sequences into a homology-split label set, and ran a first domain adaptation of ESM-C 300M on the 18.2 million centroids with LoRA (PlasticESM). To turn a ranking into DNA, we wrote a synthesis-library algorithm that encodes all 65 natural catalytic cores of the IsPETase 90% cluster in 61 oligos, about 26 kb against roughly 40 kb for a naive order, and assembles them by Golden Gate into a 2,116-variant library. The first order, two oPools covering IsPETase and LCC families at about 60 kb, went to IDT on 2026-09-28. Details, code and the measured model result are on the [Model](/dry-lab/model/) and [Dry Lab](/dry-lab/overview/) pages.




### Wet Lab

We saw the status quo in the field, and made a core decision: our assays are going to prioritize throughput and speed. So, we designed novel high-throughput assays. First, we infuse copper into a PET mesh, such that an active PETase can degrade the polymer and release the copper. E. coli carrying the copper-responsive pCusC promoter responds with two separate readouts. In the biosensor assay, pCusC drives msGFP2 expression and gives a quantitative fluorescent signal. In the selection assay, pCusC drives an antibiotic resistance marker, so only cells expressing an active enzyme grow, and survivors can be sequenced to recover the genotype. Constructs are built in pJUMP backbones by Golden Gate and expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3). Controls are FAST-PETase and IsPETase as positives, a catalytically dead PETase as the negative, and copper-only, water-only and dye-only plates for copper dependence. The BHET halo assay from the Babaian lab serves as the low-throughput positive control that already worked on this enzyme family [@chikhiLoganPlanetaryscaleGenome2024]. This year the biosensor plasmid was confirmed copper-inducible, and the selection plasmid grows at sulfadiazine ≥16 µg/mL where its negative control does not. See [Wet Lab](/wet-lab/overview/), [Engineering](/project/engineering/) and [Parts](/wet-lab/parts/).


### Hardware

Bioreactors are at the heart of deploying our enzymes. However, commercial bioreactors are either too expensive or too big to run as a parallel array to test different temperature and pH points. So, we designed reactors that can run at a chosen temperature and pH and produce a labelled (sequence, temperature, pH, activity) data point, and those points are fed back into the model for it to learn from.
The bioreactor we built is a low-cost, open-source platform on an Arduino Nano with optical density and temperature control. We took Pioreactor as the reference design, and V1 was integrated on 2026-07-30 and V2 is assembled. In its first biological run it held a 20 mL E. coli culture at 37 °C to ±0.1 °C and grew to an OD of [insert]. ### Human Practices

Plastic pollution is a global problem and we believe its solutions should be globally accessible. From the start, we designed PetaBite around a simple principle: rather than optimizing a single enzyme for a single environment, we wanted to build a discovery workflow capable of finding high-performance plastic-degrading enzymes for diverse real-world conditions. To ensure that vision was both responsible and scalable, we developed comprehensive Regulatory Oversight and Sustainability frameworks that evaluated biosafety, governance, environmental impact, accessibility, and ethical deployment.

These frameworks were informed by conversations with approximately 70 stakeholders across four continents, including researchers, regulators, wastewater operators, industry experts, ethicists, and Indigenous knowledge holders. Their feedback directly influenced our technical and deployment decisions, shaping where our technology could be used, what evidence would be required before deployment, and how enzymatic degradation should fit alongside broader prevention, recycling, and policy efforts. Beyond stakeholder engagement, we also expanded access to synthetic biology through conferences, mentorship programs, international collaborations, and campus initiatives that reached more than 800 students and community members.

[Human Practices](/beyond-the-bench/human-practices/).

### Entrepreneurship

PetaBite and PetaScale share a single goal: transforming microplastic biodegradation from a research project into a scalable commercial platform. While PetaBite served as the technical proving ground, taking the technology from 0 to 1, PetaScale is focused on taking it from 1 to n through customer discovery, commercialization, and market deployment. Our entrepreneurship team validated the opportunity through five Canadian competitions, four global competitions, and two international conferences, earning top-three placements in six events and receiving support from 17 institutional and community sponsors.

To identify the most promising market, we conducted 20 feasibility interviews with wastewater operators, engineers, researchers, and plant managers. These conversations established wastewater treatment as our beachhead market and confirmed the need for a scalable microplastic removal solution. They also informed expansion pathways into microfibre filtration, landfill leachate treatment, and textile effluent treatment, all supported by the same enzymatic platform. Early engagement with venture capital firms, startup accelerators, and industry stakeholders further validated the commercial potential of our approach.

[Entrepreneurship](/beyond-the-bench/entrepreneurship/).

### Closing the loop

We are careful to distinguish between what has already been achieved and what is still in progress. As of the freeze, Dry Lab has ranked candidates and delivered a library for testing, and the corresponding oPools have been synthesized. Wet Lab has validated its biosensor and selection systems and is currently transforming the PETase display construct after replacing an initial non-functional design. Hardware has successfully established controlled growth conditions for screening. The first model-guided screen is now underway, and the resulting activity measurements will provide the labels needed to retrain the model and inform the next round of candidate selection.

Importantly, the direction of this workflow has already been shaped by insights beyond the laboratory. The requirement for room-temperature activity emerged directly from stakeholder and commercialization conversations and now defines both the conditions Dry Lab ranks for and the conditions Hardware is designed to maintain.

## 8. Responsible by design

Three technical decisions came directly from stakeholder input. Wastewater operators made it clear that a 70 °C process would be impractical at plant scale, driving our focus on room-temperature activity. Regulators and operators also favoured a purified enzyme product over the environmental release of engineered organisms, leading us to prioritize natural enzyme scaffolds. Finally, because a contained treatment vessel is the most realistic path for regulatory approval under CEPA [@canadaOrderAddingToxic2021], we designed our deployment strategy around a contained-reactor system from the outset. The reasoning and the interview records are on [Human Practices](/beyond-the-bench/human-practices/) and [Entrepreneurship](/beyond-the-bench/entrepreneurship/).
