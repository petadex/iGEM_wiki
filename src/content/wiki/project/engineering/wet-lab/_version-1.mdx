<EngineeringStage id="design">

**A1.1 — The assay and display plasmids.** The system had to turn PET hydrolysis into a
measurable signal, which meant a sequence-verified, Registry-compatible *E. coli* vector
carrying the reporter, the selection marker and the candidate PETase.

We split it across two plasmids with compatible origins so both could be co-transformed into
the same cell: an **assay plasmid (AS)** on the **pJUMP26-1A** backbone (p15A origin,
kanamycin), and a **PETase-display plasmid (PD)** on a **pUC57-Mini-Key IIS-Free** vector
from GenScript (pUC origin, ampicillin). Assembly standardised on **BsmBI Golden Gate**
after we found the pUC19
prototype vector carried internal recognition sites for *both* BsaI and BsmBI — Type IIS
sites that survive into an assembled construct cause continuous cutting and re-ligation, so
we removed the conflict before finalising. We then built the required Type IIS sites
directly into the ordered DNA rather than adding them later by PCR, which cut downstream
cloning steps and the chances of sequence error.

Part choices, each with its reason:

| Choice | Over | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **dfrB10** | dfrA1 | dfrA1 closely resembles native *E. coli* DHFR, risking recombination with the chromosome. dfrB10 is a small Type II DHFR, structurally and evolutionarily distinct; BLAST against the *E. coli* genome returned no significant similarity. |
| **sul1 and sul2** | picking one | Both carried in parallel so their performance could be compared directly. |
| **msGFP2** | mCherry | A monomeric reporter avoids the oligomerisation artifacts of multimeric ones. |
| **J23102** | J23119, J23106 | Reliably strong signal without unnecessary metabolic burden. PETase expression runs from a separate promoter on PD, so this one was tuned for the controls. |
| **A validated terminator** | random DNA | Random sequence does not guarantee transcriptional silence — roughly 10% of random sequences act as weak promoters in *E. coli*. |

</EngineeringStage>

<EngineeringStage id="build">

We fixed **three validation steps before building anything**: sequence verification for
every construct, a copper induction curve, and an antibiotic kill curve — with positive and
negative controls required in every assay, cloning workflows the only exception.

Primers were ordered **2026-06-30** as a dry 96-well plate for roughly $500, about half the
cost of individual tubes. The order included Gibson assembly oligonucleotides as a
contingency in case Golden Gate failed. We declined PAGE purification, which would have
added about $80 per primer, because the long primers were for PCR-based overhang addition
rather than sequencing.

Benchmark controls came from the Brown Lab through an Addgene MTA reviewed **2026-06-09**;
*E. coli* strains carrying **IsPETase** and **FAST-PETase** arrived **2026-06-08**.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

Transformation was validated against a pUC19 positive control on **2026-06-29**. That same
week **IsPETT, pJUMP26, pRBS and pEFTerm** transformed successfully, while **EcCO WT** and
**pTac** produced only two colonies each. Sequencing confirmed the **IsPETase W159H**
construct on **2026-07-06**, giving us a verified benchmark variant.

**Then verification started failing in a way we did not expect.** On **2026-07-16**
sequencing returned no plasmid at all despite healthy colony growth and measurable DNA after
miniprep. A second construct did the same on **2026-07-20** — the nanodrop showed sufficient
DNA, but sequencing could not generate a consensus. By early August several cloned constructs
appeared empty despite otherwise successful cloning.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

**The bottleneck was plasmid preparation, not assembly.** Most failures traced back to a
~20 mL "mini-midi hybrid" workflow that routinely produced DNA below the sequencing vendor's
recommended threshold of about 200 ng/µL. Short-read verification had occasionally returned
partial data, which masked the problem for weeks.

We switched to **whole-plasmid nanopore sequencing** to remove the ambiguity. The first
runs, on **2026-08-11**, had few reads and genomic DNA contamination: msGFP2 and sul2 were
readable, while **sul1 simply needed a fresh miniprep** — a read-quality problem, not an
assembly failure. That distinction is the whole point of the cycle, and we only had it
because whole-plasmid data showed us the construct rather than a failed consensus.

A fresh prep settled it on **2026-08-15**: **AS-msGFP2 confirmed** (carrying a single
base-pair mismatch inherited from the backbone) and **AS-sul1 confirmed**, while **AS-dfr
and AS-sul2 had to be redone**. Of the three resistance genes attempted in the AS vector,
**only the sul1 assembly succeeded, so all subsequent selection testing used sul1.**

The design change this forced: we had been treating failed sequencing as evidence of failed
assembly. Whole-plasmid sequencing showed the real issue was yield. **Whole-plasmid
verification became mandatory before any construct could enter an assay** — which is the
practice the October rebuild in A1.2 depended on.

<Callout tone="note" title="Why the verification change mattered">

Had we kept reading short-read failures as assembly failures, **sul1 — the one resistance
construct that actually assembled — would have been discarded**, and the selection assay
would have had no working marker. The constructs that genuinely needed rebuilding, dfr and
sul2, were identified in the same run. Whole-plasmid verification did not just catch a bad
prep; it prevented a correct construct from being thrown away.

</Callout>
