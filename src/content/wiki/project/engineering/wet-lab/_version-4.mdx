<EngineeringStage id="design">

**A2.2 — The detection circuit and its medium.** On **2026-05-31** we selected **CopA over
CusC**, because a previous iGEM team had reported success with the promoter, because it is
regulated by CueR, and because increasing CusC sensitivity appeared to require knocking out
endogenous copper-transport genes. We confirmed that on **2026-06-19**, noting that CopA
responds to **cytoplasmic** copper — meaning copper must cross the inner membrane before
detection, which in principle reduces cheater phenotypes and allows detection as low as
0.1 mM — whereas CusC responds to **periplasmic** copper and is generally associated with
higher, near-toxic concentrations.

On **2026-07-04** we nevertheless finalised **pCusC** as the assay promoter, on the grounds
that it has a more established literature base and stronger prior characterisation. Both
decisions are recorded because the reversal is the interesting part: we traded a mechanistic
argument for a characterisation one.

**The medium mattered as much as the promoter.** Standard LB agar contains enough thymine
and thymidine for cells to bypass the folate-pathway inhibition that both trimethoprim and
sulfonamides rely on. To preserve selection pressure we adopted **cation-adjusted
Mueller-Hinton agar**, the CLSI-recommended medium for trimethoprim and sulfonamide
susceptibility testing, on **2026-06-26**.

Published pCusC characterisation set our expectations before we tested anything: detectable
fluorescence above background at roughly 39 µM copper, a quantifiable response from about
118 µM, stable high-level induction at 500 µM over 16 hours, and toxicity at 1000 µM where
fluorescence declines after about 4 hours. **These are published values used to guide assay
design, not our measurements.**

On **2026-08-11** we designed a plate grid with **eight copper concentrations** on one axis
and **four construct-selection conditions** on the other. The purpose was not just to find
the pCusC activation threshold but to separate genuine promoter induction from the
alternative failure modes that produce similar-looking plates: copper toxicity, promoter
leakiness, weak resistance expression, and overly stringent selection.

**A failure worth showing, 2026-08-03.** We prepared Mueller-Hinton plates spanning a range
of copper concentrations and **the copper gradient did not behave as expected**. We
suspected the medium and commissioned two literature reviews: one on Mueller-Hinton agar in
trimethoprim and sulfonamide susceptibility testing, and one on its effect on copper
bioavailability through phosphate chelation and precipitation. The second, returned
**2026-08-05**, found little directly relevant literature because the assay design is novel.
**This question remains open.**

</EngineeringStage>

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

We chose Mueller-Hinton because it is the standard medium for trimethoprim and sulfonamide
susceptibility testing. But **a medium that is optimal for antibiotic selection may be
suboptimal for copper bioavailability**, and those two requirements are in tension in an
assay that needs both at once. We could not resolve the trade-off: neither our own data nor
the available literature established how Mueller-Hinton agar affects copper availability in
this system.

That is the honest state of the detection circuit. The promoter responds to copper and the
resistance gene expresses — both shown above — but the medium that makes the selection arm
work may be the same medium suppressing the copper signal the biosensor arm depends on.

What changed going into A3: we added a readout path that does not depend on copper chemistry
at all, and began testing the two surviving formats as one integrated assay.
