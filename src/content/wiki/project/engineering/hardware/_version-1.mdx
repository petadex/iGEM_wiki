<EngineeringStage id="design">

**C1.1 — Requirements before parts.** Because iGEM Toronto is the only iGEM team in eastern
North America with a dedicated Hardware subteam, we had no internal precedent to build on.
So we bought and assembled two commercial **Pioreactors** as reference builds first — one in
sprint 1 (9–22 March) with its components documented, and two connected together by the end
of sprint 3 (6–30 April).

**Requirements came from five interviews, not from our own assumptions:** the Pioreactor
CEO, an Eppendorf bioreactor sales representative, Dr. Mackay on microcontroller
architecture, and our own Wet Lab and Dry Lab leads as internal users.

The resulting document is **quantified**, which is the point of it:

| Category | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Heating | Ambient to 50 °C without melting plastic components, with accurate readings |
| Mixing | 100 to 1,000 RPM, and the stir bar must not affect OD readings |
| Pumping | At least two pumps in and out, no leaks, 1–2 mL dosage range |
| OD | Accurate across the useful range, not disrupted by the stirring vortex |
| Power | Run the array from a single laboratory circuit, IP-rated against wet-lab splashes |
| Ergonomics | 80% of routine tasks single-handed, autoclave-compatible materials, tool-less teardown under 2 minutes |
| Assembly | Every contact-surface component autoclavable or single-use |
| Network | The full array within lab bandwidth |

**Power was the constraint that shaped the architecture.** The requirement reads: total
current draw for 96 reactors must stay under the safe load limit for a 120 V circuit in the
Donnelly Centre — **a 15 A breaker derated to 12 A, giving a 1,440 W ceiling** for the whole
array. (Our own requirement note writes the breaker rating as "15A(?)", so the derated 12 A
figure is the one the budget was actually built against.)

That single number drove three decisions, recorded as sub-objectives against it:
**scheduling** heating and mixing rather than running them simultaneously to keep overall
wattage down; **separating** the heating and mixing power lines from the microcontrollers to
protect them from voltage spikes; and **centralising** power in a high-capacity shared DC
supply to avoid 96 individual wall adapters.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

Two architectural decisions came directly out of the reference builds and the interviews.

**First, we replaced the Raspberry Pi with an ESP32.** Cameron Davidson-Pilon named his own
regrets in designing the Pioreactor — exposed wiring, choosing the Raspberry Pi over a more
user-friendly microcontroller, and the **1:1 ratio between microcontroller and reactor**.
Dr. Mackay made the same point independently: a Raspberry Pi provides excessive computing
power for one bioreactor. The ESP32 offered a simpler bill of materials, more modular
hardware and easier power distribution while still supporting everything required —
stirring through 12 PWM channels, heater control through 2 low-frequency PWM outputs, and
temperature and optical-density monitoring through ADC and I²C.

**Second, on 2026-05-28 we finalised a single-vessel workflow.** Each reactor would grow
cells, induce expression, apply selection and run degradation experiments in the same
vessel, eliminating the need for a separate culture-production system or chemostat.

That second decision defined the core engineering challenge. The workflow requires **three
temperature regimes** — growth at 37 °C, induction at 25 °C, degradation at 50–70 °C — with
in-vessel sterilisation at 70 °C, while the project's target enzymes ideally remain stable
above 80 °C.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

**The thermal requirements and the power budget could not both be met at the original scale.**
On **2026-06-29** we resolved the conflict by splitting the heating load: **individual
reactors would heat only to 40 °C, while a centralised heating system would provide the
60–70 °C degradation environment.**

As part of the same decision, we **cut the design from 96 reactors to 8**. This was the
largest scope reduction in the hardware track and the change that made the platform feasible
to build, power and validate within the project's constraints. It is also the decision we
would defend most strongly: the alternative was 96 units that could not be powered or
thermally driven to spec.

**What changed going into C1.2:** the architecture shifted from 96 reactors to 8, from
networked units to USB serial communication, and from a hardware-first design to one that
treated the live software dashboard as a core subsystem from the outset.
