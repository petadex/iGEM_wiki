<EngineeringStage id="design">

**B2.4 — LOBSTER, the library design algorithm.** We framed this as a **sequence-purchasing
optimisation problem**: represent the natural catalytic diversity within a 90% identity
cluster using the fewest synthesised oligonucleotides possible. The approach exploits
degenerate codons at positions where cluster members differ, then partitions the resulting
design into fragments compatible with Golden Gate assembly.

We first formalised it on **2026-05-26** as choosing degenerate-codon oligo libraries that
maximise recovery of natural wild-type sequences while satisfying constraints on RNA
stability, codon composition and vendor ordering limits. We reviewed existing approaches —
an integer-programming coverage formulation, SwiftLib, CoLiDe and DeCoiL — but adopted none
directly, because none accommodated our full set of assembly constraints.

The algorithm enforces these simultaneously:

- **No BsmBI recognition sites** within any fragment, including sites created inadvertently
  across fragment junctions.
- **Four-nucleotide overhangs** that are unique between assembly buckets, shared within
  buckets, and non-palindromic.
- **Reserved backbone junction overhangs** of CGGA and GGTG.

The CGGA choice does double duty. Because CGGA encodes the terminal glycine of the GS
linker, reserving that overhang **preserves the reading frame by design** rather than
through padding or post-hoc correction. We confirmed with Wet Lab on **2026-09-11** that the
resulting assembly strategy introduces no additional residues before or after the coding
sequence.

</EngineeringStage>

<EngineeringStage id="build">

We implemented the algorithm with a **bookend architecture**, finalised **2026-09-10**:
outer fragments carry a BsmBI site, spacer sequence and backbone-compatible overhang, while
internal fragments carry a recognition site, spacer and the appropriate half of the shared
two-nucleotide junction sequence. The output is an **order-ready CSV** containing all oligo
designs and the assembly metadata required for synthesis.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

We evaluated the method on the **IsPETase and LCC 90%-identity clusters** and measured what
it saved against synthesising every sequence individually.

The designed library covers **1,744 sequences at roughly 45% of the individual-synthesis
cost**. In nucleotides, the design saves **13,874 nt on the IsPETase cluster and 6,104 nt on
LCC**. The algorithm completes **within 20 minutes** across all fragment counts, so the
design step is not a bottleneck in the order cycle.

To verify correctness rather than assume it, we built a validation pipeline on
**2026-08-27**. It checks Golden Gate junction integrity, forbidden BsmBI sites in both
orientations including sites created across junctions, recovery of every intended natural
catalytic core, codon integrity, absence of internal stop codons, compliance with
library-size and nucleotide limits, and reproducibility from a fixed random seed.

As an additional structural sanity check, we folded two independent sets of 100 designed
constructs with **ESMFold2** on **2026-09-10** and **2026-09-11**.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

Two design questions were resolved through discussion with Wet Lab rather than in isolation.

**Fragment lengths vary across library members**, because cluster representatives contain
insertions and deletions relative to one another. Wet Lab identified this as a potential
assembly risk on **2026-09-09**; we reviewed it jointly and resolved it on a design call on
**2026-09-10**, allowing variable-length fragments to remain in the final design.

**The codon table does not match the expression strain.** The algorithm uses codon
frequencies from the Kazusa *E. coli* **W3110** table (taxid 316407, 4,332 CDSs over
1,372,057 codons, accessed 2026-08-27), while expression experiments use
BL21(DE3). We judged the difference unlikely to materially affect library construction and
did not pursue further optimisation — **though the choice was never formally benchmarked**,
and we note that rather than claim it was validated.

The broader lesson is that **assembly and purchasing constraints matter as much as sequence
diversity**. A library that maximises biological coverage but cannot be synthesised,
assembled or validated is not useful. By building those constraints directly into the design
algorithm rather than filtering for them afterwards, we cut synthesis cost while preserving
complete coverage of known natural catalytic-core diversity in the target cluster.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Three claims this cycle does not make">

**There is no cost-reduction iteration.** One benchmark exists, on one cluster, on one date.
There is no second version of the algorithm and no re-benchmark on the same input, so there
is no before-and-after. **The cost metric is not dollars.** On 2026-09-29 the algorithm's
author declined to estimate a dollar figure, on the grounds that we have no sound basis for
per-sequence cost estimates over PETadex — nucleotides and oligos are the units used here.
**No link between HMM quality and synthesis cost is documented**; the only HMM-and-cost
connection in our records is that curation was front-loaded so a Logan search costing
thousands would not run against an unfinished corpus.

</Callout>
