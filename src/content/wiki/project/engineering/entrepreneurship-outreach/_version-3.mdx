<EngineeringStage id="design">

**D3 — The UofT Microplastics Conference.** With support from the U of T Trash Team, we
designed a free public event tracing plastic pollution from how widespread it is in the
environment, through its effects on human health, to the policies meant to address it.

We centred the day on **freshwater microplastics** — an urgent and under-monitored problem
with direct Toronto relevance. The Great Lakes hold 84% of North America's surface
freshwater and roughly 20% of the world's, and researchers have found microplastics in every
one of them.

The design goal was to put researchers, the public and decision-makers in the same room,
under the theme **"Rethink plastic,"** built around two questions: what happens to plastic
once it enters the environment, and what we can realistically do about it.

**Access was a design constraint, not an afterthought.** Admission was free and open to
anyone, and our call for abstracts invited undergraduate and graduate students from every
discipline, so that students with no prior conference experience had a genuine chance to
present and members of the public with no research background would feel welcome.

Initial targets were ambitious: **250–500 attendees and $50,000 in sponsorship.**

</EngineeringStage>

<EngineeringStage id="build">

An **eight-person team** drawn from three subteams built the event. By **2026-06-15** they
had finished a proposal of more than 40 pages combining event overview, sponsorship package
and outreach strategy into a single plan. The website went live **2026-07-20** and the
speaker lineup was finalised **2026-08-11**: Kara Lavender Law, Chelsea Rochman, Madeleine
Milne, Miriam Diamond and Karen Wirsig.

**Then the venue problem.** D-Hall declined the event on **2026-06-15**. On **2026-08-03**
the university's booking system would not allow a Bahen Centre reservation past August. Our
plan had assumed a venue; without one, speaker recruitment and sponsor outreach had to go on
hold.

We adopted a clear fallback rule: **if a bigger venue could not be confirmed in time, we
would announce a smaller one on schedule rather than keep waiting.** Expected attendance
shrank with each new constraint — from 500 to 300 on **2026-07-06**, settling at 200–300 —
until **2026-08-10**, when rooms 1140 and 1160 in the Bahen Centre were confirmed free of
charge.

The programme was then reworked on speaker feedback: Prof. Miriam Diamond was concerned
sessions ran too long, so on **2026-08-17** we cut talks to 20 minutes and lengthened the
coffee breaks to leave more room for discussion and networking.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

**The test was running the event, on 2026-09-19.**

**Over 200 people registered**, and **15 scientific posters** were submitted from **four
universities**. Attendees included undergraduate and graduate students, faculty,
researchers, environmental advocates and members of the public — many attending a research
conference for the first time.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

The learning came from three directions, and the first one changed the event itself.

**From the Trash Team, before the event.** During the May cleanups, Trash Team members asked
whether technologies like enzymatic degradation **risk distracting from reducing plastic
use**. That question stayed with us through planning. It is why we built the programme
around prevention, policy and community action alongside technical solutions, and why we
presented PetaBite as **one approach among many** rather than as the solution. In our own
Scientific Solutions session we framed it explicitly as a complement to prevention rather
than a replacement, and said where the project could fall short.

**From attendees, during it.** The audience split cleanly by what they pushed on.
Researchers asked about enzyme activity and how we measure degradation. Policy and advocacy
voices asked how an engineered enzyme would be regulated and **what evidence regulators
would need before approving it for use in water systems**. Members of the public asked
whether an engineered enzyme could be used safely near the environment. We explained our
science, and in return were pushed on the regulatory, environmental and community realities
the work has to address.

**From running it.** The organising leads recorded that the hardest part at this scale was
keeping a growing number of people, ideas and moving pieces aligned as plans changed, which
pushed them into a bridging role between the two teams: *"Dividing up responsibilities is
the easy part. The harder and more valuable work is bringing different strengths together
around something neither team could have built the same way alone."*

**We also corrected how we described the collaboration three times, each time narrowing the
claim to match the evidence.** On **2026-06-05** the Trash Team's role changed from
"partnership" to "support". On **2026-08-05** the event was reframed as one iGEM Toronto
started rather than one created jointly. On **2026-08-21** "under the guidance of" became
"with support from". That is a small discipline, but it is the same discipline this whole
page runs on.

The event also sharpened our economics: the prototype trading cards handed out cost about
**$1.25 each** to make, against a post-event target of under **$1.00** and a planned retail
price of **$5 per pack**. An outreach giveaway became a revenue line with real numbers
attached.
