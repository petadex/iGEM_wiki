**D1 — External technical review.** People outside our team changed our technical designs
repeatedly this season. This cycle records that as an engineering process rather than as
outreach: we looked for reviewers who would judge the platform **by the standards of their
own fields, not ours** — a hardware manufacturer, a biomedical engineering professor,
wastewater engineers, and a journal editor.

We hold this to one standard: **if a conversation did not lead to something we can point to,
we do not count it as evidence.**

For context, Dr. Artem Babaian is our principal investigator and Jonas Lehar and Kat Roth
are club advisors and lab staff. All three know the project closely, which makes their
feedback valuable for engineering decisions but tells us little about how people outside the
project see the work. They are internal review, and we do not count them among our external
stakeholders.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

Each change below traces to a single conversation, in the reviewer's own recorded terms.

**Cameron Davidson-Pilon → the controller and the architecture.** He named his own regrets
in designing the Pioreactor: exposed wiring, choosing the Raspberry Pi over a more
user-friendly microcontroller (**ESP32**), and the **1:1 ratio between microcontroller and
reactor**. That drove our microcontroller testing and our requirements on parallelisability
and on shielding electronics from water. He also confirmed a Pi Pico could replace the full
Raspberry Pi and that Pi, STM and Arduino perform comparably for our task, which reduced the
controller choice to cost and pin count. His warning about custom-PCB lead time moved us to
off-the-shelf boards and the **StemmaQT** connector standard — which is exactly the
connector that later fixed the loose-jumper failure in the OD calibration run.

**Dr. Mackay → power architecture.** A Raspberry Pi provides excessive computing power for
one bioreactor, and **scheduling or a separate power supply** makes mixing, stirring and
heating less energy-intensive. That is the origin of isolating those circuits from the
compute lines.

**Prof. Rasha Maal-Bared → where the system is deployed.** **60 to 90% of PET microplastics
end up in WWTP sludge rather than the water stream.** Digesters run at mesophilic
temperatures. She recommended **sidestream deployment** for optimal contact between PETase
and microplastics. She also made an operability point we designed to: **plant operators, not
engineers, manage day-to-day processes, so design for operators.**

**Prof. Milica Radisic → the reactor scale.** She judged the 40 mL vessel an order of
magnitude too large for the intermediate screen and pushed us toward a lower-volume
screening format, splitting one instrument into two tiers. She also agreed to monthly design
reviews and began sourcing a thesis student for the screening tier — the one review that
became a standing relationship rather than a single conversation.

Two further advisors produced changes outside the reactor itself. **Sophia Young** observed
that small particles pass physical filters, so the system belongs in the **biological
treatment stage**, and argued the strongest IP path is to patent the **ML process** — the
algorithm and optimisation — rather than individual enzymes. **Mary Mohamad** established
that student IP rights are not automatic and university ownership must be settled before
commercialisation.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

**Across every one of these reviews, outside experts corrected our engineering and
deployment assumptions and never our biology.** None of them questioned whether PETases
work. Every change was to how the system is built, where it runs, or how big it is.

That is exactly the category of mistake a team that spends most of its time at the bench is
least able to catch on its own, and it is the argument for seeking out reviewers who do not
share your assumptions. The controller choice, the power architecture, the deployment point
within a treatment plant, and the vessel scale were all things we believed we had settled
internally.
