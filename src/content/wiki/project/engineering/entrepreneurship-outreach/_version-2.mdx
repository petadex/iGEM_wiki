<EngineeringStage id="design">

**D2 — From a voted list to four verticals and a sized market.** The question this cycle has
to answer is who pays, what they are paying for, and what evidence we have that they will.

We started from an internal vote that narrowed the field to four segments: wastewater, oil
and gas, waste management, and landfills.

On **2026-07-01** we adopted the governance rule that makes the rest of this cycle legible:
**one owner per vertical**; an owner who judges their vertical infeasible may pivot it
provided they tell the team lead; and **every change is appended to the record rather than
overwriting what came before.** That last clause is the only reason this cycle can be
written at all — without it the pivots would be invisible.

</EngineeringStage>

<EngineeringStage id="build">

Two things were built in parallel: the **vertical list** and the **price-anchored market
model**.

The list churned — three verticals at launch, seven with owners, and finally **a WWTP
beachhead plus three adjacent verticals**: textile effluent, landfill leachate, and
filtration. Each of the four received a full report covering technical feasibility and SWOT,
market size, barriers to entry, and a competitor matrix.

**The model was built from a price rather than a top-down total.** PetaScale sells enzymatic
PET removal at **$15.75 per megalitre** of plant influent treated — set deliberately at
roughly 35% of the **$45.26/ML** Region of Peel already spends on process chemicals across
G.E. Booth and Clarkson (2023), so it fits inside an existing budget line instead of
requiring a new one. Variable cost is **$3.02/ML** ($1.62 enzyme, $1.40 cold-chain delivery,
dosing and Py-GC/MS verification); fully loaded cost is **$7.51/ML**, a roughly 2.1× markup.
Pilots bill at **$80,000 per pilot-year** and convert to full contracts once performance is
validated.

<EngineeringStage id="test">

**The vertical list tested itself through its own change log, and the reasons are the
result:**

| Date | Change | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2026-06-08 | Started with three verticals: wastewater, bioremediation, detergent | The market subteam launched |
| 2026-06-09 | Dropped in-wash detergent, added post-wash filtration | Our enzyme degrades polyester, so a detergent containing it could damage polyester clothing — and we could not rely on people reading a warning label |
| 2026-07-07 | Expanded to seven verticals, each with an owner | Restructured under the new one-owner rule |
| 2026-07-27 | Dropped oil and gas, and degreasers | **Not recorded** |
| 2026-08-17 | Narrowed again | **Not recorded** |
| 2026-08-24 | Dropped consumer drinking-water filtration, added industrial laundering | The consumer market was already crowded with established players, and drinking-water products need heavy regulatory approval |

**The market tested as:** a total addressable market of **$1.37 billion a year** — plants in
the United States, Canada and Western Europe treat 87.3 billion m³ of wastewater annually at
our price. The serviceable market is **$69.1 million a year**: Canada's 4.38 billion m³ of
secondary and tertiary treatment across 11 provinces and territories, with Ontario, Quebec,
Alberta and British Columbia holding 89.6% of the volume. The obtainable market is
**$15.80 million in annual recurring revenue** — 24 plants across seven authorities treating
1.0 billion m³, or **22.9% of the Canadian market**, by Year 4.

The beachhead itself was validated through **20 interviews** with operators, researchers and
technical staff across Ontario, Alberta and Newfoundland, which confirmed the approach fits
existing plant workflows.

<EngineeringStage id="learn">

**Both cuts with recorded reasons came down to consumer behaviour and market structure
rather than technical problems** — a category of finding only this track produces. The
enzyme worked fine in a detergent; the problem was that we could not control what consumers
washed with it.

**The two changes without recorded reasons are a failure of our own governance rule**, which
existed precisely to capture them. We are stating that rather than quietly presenting a
clean four-vertical story.

**The deeper change was methodological: we abandoned top-down sizing altogether.** The
chemical-spend figure stopped being a way to *size* the market and became the way to
*justify the price* — showing that plants already budget for chemical treatment, so the
money exists. That is why the surviving model starts at $15.75/ML and builds upward through
named authorities and named plants, rather than taking a percentage of a large number. The
projections are explicitly scoped to the WWTP vertical alone, use published LCC-ICCG
benchmarks until our own pilot data replaces them, and hold prices and costs flat.

**Honest gaps, stated rather than omitted.** No customer has given us a letter of intent or
a letter of support. Our own planning document set a bar of twenty customer interviews, at
least one letter of intent, and a score of 40% or higher on a product-market-fit survey —
**we never ran the survey**. We flagged per-unit enzyme production cost as the key gap on
2026-04-29, again in May, and again in August, and never put an independent number on it. On
2026-10-04, two days after declaring this work complete, we recognised that a business model
canvas was effectively expected, and we had not built one.
