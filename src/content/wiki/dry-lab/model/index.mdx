## Motivation

{/*
  ~200 words. Answers Q1. No figure.
  Why a model is needed at all: the question it has to answer.
*/}

#### Current landscape of plastic-degrading enzymes
Plastic-degrading enzymes offer a promising route to bioremediating plastic waste, but only a small
number have been characterised. The *Plastics-Active Enzymes Database* (PAZy) [@buchholz2022pazy]
catalogues experimentally validated plastic-degrading enzymes, and holds just 495 of them.
*PlasticDB* [@gambarini2022plasticdb] adds organism and environmental metadata, but records only 875
plastic-degrading organisms, active across a relatively narrow set of environmental conditions. 
This known set is a small, biased sample of what exists in nature.

**To discover and engineer ultra-efficient plastic-degrading enzymes, we need to look beyond the 
existing datasets, into the sequence and environmental landscape that public sequencing data already holds.**

#### What we are building from
The advent of artificial intelligence in biology may offer a path to navigating these landscapes, but existing datasets
are far too small to train such models. In prior work, our supervising lab (RNAlab) built the *PETadex*: 47.2 million
non-redundant plastic-degrading enzyme homologs, mined from *Logan*, an assembly of 38 million experiments held in the Sequence
Read Archive [@chikhi2024logan]. Screening randomly sampled *Is*PETase homologs from *PETadex* on BHET yielded two novel enzymes more
active than FAST-PETase, the field's engineered benchmark.

**Random sampling alone was enough to beat the benchmark, but the original *PETadex* lacked the
sequence annotations needed to effectively navigate the sequence landscape.**

## What Our Model Does

{/*

**Project Goal:** Build the data, and the evaluation, needed for a model that predicts PETase activity under different conditions, to 
accelerate the discovery of ultra-efficient plastic-degrading enzymes.

#### PETadex V2: The largest database for plastic-degrading enzyme homologs
We built *PETadex V2*, a complete remake of the PETadex dataset from the ground up: modernizing the enzymes from
2024 to 2026 and improving data curation steps. We retrieved 1,740,282,847 non-redundant plastic-degrading enzyme homologs,
filtered to a set of 291,922,243 complete proteins using our catalytically bound PETadex-HMMs. From the Sequence Read Archive
metadata of each source sample, we annotated every sequence with the conditions it came from: pH, temperature, salinity
and environment type. We also computed biochemical properties, predicted signal peptides and mapped domain architectures.
Everything is freely available at *petadex.net*

#### Mining the literature for plastic-degrading activity
Sequences and conditions alone cannot predict PETase activity; the model must learn from experimentally validated
activities. The issue is that almost all of this data is locked in the text, tables and figures of published papers. We
built an LLM pipeline to extract it and ran it over 4,000 papers, recovering 51,455 activity measurements across hundreds
of conditions and a diverse set of plastics. To benchmark the pipeline, over 20 iGEM Toronto members read those papers and
extracted PETase activities from 61 of them by hand, a cumulative 450 hours.

#### Predicting Plastic-Degrading Activity
Now that we have the largest database of plastic-degrading enzyme homologs to date, we can now train a model that can understand
the plastic-degrading enzyme sequence and condition landscape.

We adapted the ESM-C protein language model [@candido2026esmfold2] to PETadex, producing *PlasticESM*, and then tested whether its representations predict measured activity. We held that test to two rules: related enzymes never sit on both sides of a train/test split, and a model must beat simple controls such as sequence length. With the labels available today, no model clears that bar yet, and a conventional random split would have hidden this from us. The bottleneck is the labels, not the corpus, so the next wet-lab round will be chosen to fill the gaps: the clusters the model is least certain about, among those whose environmental metadata looks promising.

#### Training on Tens of Thousands of In-House Datapoints
Training a robust model takes tens to hundreds of thousands of sequence–activity pairs, but
synthesizing that many unique genes is far beyond any iGEM budget. We addressed this by developing
LOBSTER (Library Optimization for Bioprospecting Sequences Through Economical Recombination).
LOBSTER generates a library of oligos that, when synthesized and assembled, capture the variation
of more sequences than were ordered. It utilizes the sequence depth of the PETadex,
Golden Gate assembly of fragments, and the encoding of degenerate bases to greatly reduce synthesis costs.
Using LOBSTER, we synthesized a library of 1,744 sequences at 45% of the cost of synthesizing them individually.
These are from the *Is*PETase and LCC enzyme clusters and will be screened by the Wet Lab to validate the model.
The data generated by the Wet Lab team will then be used to further train the model, improving its predictive capacity.

**Why PETadex matters:** PETadex V2 is the largest resource of plastic-degrading enzyme homologs
assembled to date, and pairing it with validated activities gives ML tools the scale they have lacked
for enzyme discovery so far. Nothing in the pipeline is specific to PETases: the same
combination of homolog mining, condition annotation and literature-scraped activity data can be
used for other enzyme families and other reaction conditions.

### Background and Overview
The creation of the PETadex in prior work [@chikhi2024logan] and the creation of PETadex V2 here, relies on the foundation 
built by our supervising lab (RNAlab) through the *Logan* project. *Logan* is the assembly of the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), 
the leading public repository for global sequencing experiments (38M experiments). Researchers often require only a little 
portion of the total sequencing dataset, and thus will not run a genome assembly algorithm over the sample due to its expensive 
nature. Therefore a large portion of the SRA is unassembled, rendering it difficult to use. *Logan* assembles the SRA, 
compressing the 87.0 Petabases of raw data into 1.8 Petabases of searchable contigs. 

We searched for plastic-degrading enzyme homologs through a sequence alignment search against the *Logan* assembly, retrieving over
1.7 billion putative plastic-degrading enzyme homologs. The well studied polyethylene terephthalate (PET)-degrading enzyme, *Is*PETase, 
was discovered using similar means [@yoshida2016]. The researchers identified a bacterium, *Ideonella sakaiensis*, able to degrade
PET using an unknown enzyme. They discovered the identity of this enzyme by performing an alignment search of a known plastic-degrading
enzyme called TfH against the *I. sakaiensis* genome. Our dataset generation pipeline mirrors these methods, but lacks a key feature:
we do not know which genomes belong to plastic-degrading organisms.

The first set of annotations of the PETadex is which CATH domain these proteins are from. We assigned
each PETadex-PAZy protein a structural label from CATH, the standard system for sorting protein domains
according to their 3D structural folds [@sillitoe2021cath; @waman2024cath].

Each enzyme was labelled by comparing its predicted structure to known structures [@lau2023merizo; @vankempen2024foldseek], and by matching its sequence 
to both known CATH [@lewis2018gene3d] and Pfam [@paysanlafosse2025pfam] sequence models. We then made a phylogenetic tree of the dataset, which
shows that the distinct PETadex domains are unalignable to each other, forming independent roots. Additionally,
the tree demonstrates that most of our database is dominated by α/β-hydrolases, which include several landmark
enzymes such as *Is*PETase [@yoshida2016] and LCC [@sulaiman2012lcc].

### NCBI-nr Homology Expansion

{/*

PETadex-PAZy is far too small to train on, so we used it as a starting point rather than as the dataset itself. We
searched its centroids against the NCBI nr database with DIAMOND [@buchfink2021diamond]. All 411 PETadex-PAZy
centroids went into the search. The search returned 42,053,081 hits; keeping the best hit per unique sequence, applying an
E-value cutoff of 1 × 10⁻⁵ and removing PETadex-PAZy self-hits leaves 7,825,107 non-redundant sequences, spanning
13,209,543 nr accessions.

Sequence similarity alone does not make a candidate, so we then ran the 53 catalytic HMMs against those 7,825,107
sequences. This gave 7,562,397 sequences with a significant hit, of which 6,764,617 carried a complete
hit: the matched HMM range covered every catalytic residue position. Clustering the catalytic domain ranges of the
complete hits at 90% amino acid identity left 2,740,458 centroids. These centroids, each a catalytic domain rather than
a whole protein, are what we take into Logan.

### Seed MSAs and Catalytic HMMs

A homolog is only a candidate plastic-degrading enzyme if it carries the catalytic machinery, so we searched Logan with
the catalytic domain alone rather than with whole proteins. This required profile HMMs that capture both the boundaries
of the plastic-degrading domain and the catalytic residues within it.

Each HMM was seeded from an alignment of PETadex-PAZy sequences and their closest nr homologs, admitting a sequence only
if it carried the residues of the catalytic mechanism, which we annotated from the literature. A single
alignable component can hold more than one mechanism, so we segmented the 46 PETadex-PAZy components by mechanism,
resolving them into 53 seed alignments. Each seed was then expanded over four iterations of a search analogous to
jackhmmer [@johnson2010jackhmmer], in which the hits of one round build the HMM used by the next: an nr homolog advanced only if it
retained every canonical catalytic residue. The 53 HMMs that came out of this are what we use to find and delimit
catalytic domains in everything the searches below return.

Searching nr finds the homologs that have been deposited; Logan reaches the sequencing data that was never assembled
into them. We searched the 2,740,458 catalytic domain centroids against Logan contigs, recovering
5,573,419,026 hits. Calling an open reading frame around each one, and splitting those broken by an internal

complete hits carrying every catalytic residue position. Together these searches make up PETadex V2, and we keep the

from; **PETadex-nr**, the homologs recovered from NCBI nr; and **PETadex-Logan**, those recovered from Logan.

## Sequence Annotation

{/*

Now that we have a large database of putative plastic-degrading enzymes, the sequences alone tell us little about what is in it, where the sequences come from, or how they can be used. Sequence annotation fills that gap. We add several additional layers to tell us more about the sequence. We can figure out the environment its sample came from, its domain architecture, its predicted 3D structure, and where in the cell it is likely to be sent.

The environment alone carries information that isn't obvious from the sequence. Knowing that a sequence comes from a hot-spring sample, for example, suggests a high temperature, and often an unusual pH and salinity. Each layer gives the model something new to learn from, and together they describe the world each enzyme comes from, beyond its one-dimensional sequence. Not every annotation is available for every sequence, so for each layer we report how much of the database it covers.

### Biosample Metadata Annotations

A plastic-degrading enzyme is only useful if we know where its host organism lives and what conditions it tolerates. We started from 932 validated plastic-degrading organisms and 2,535 organism-polymer records in PlasticDB [@gambarini2022plasticdb] (Figure 4A) and found that only 527 records (21%) carry an enzyme sequence, from 77% for PET down to 0.9% for LDPE (Figure 4B). We then added laboratory growth tests for 455 BacDive [@reimer2022bacdive] strains: 44% of 421 grow at 25°C or below (Figure 4C), 71% of 75 at pH 9 or above (Figure 4D) and 50% of 155 at 5% NaCl or above (Figure 4E). Environment classes for 646 BioSamples are led by soil and sediment (Figure 4F), yet two sources agree on the class for only 46% of organisms. Together these results set assay conditions of 25°C, pH 8 to 9 and added NaCl, and give a first panel of 27 cold-active strains with a protein and a closed genome.

### Localization and Signal Peptides

SignalP 6.0 was run on all PETadex ORFs to identify the secreted enzymes within the database, to support enzyme selection for in vitro activity testing and to produce cleaved mature sequences for more accurate downstream analysis. Bacterial enzymes primarily employ the Sec (Secretory) and Tat (Twin-Arginine Translocation) pathways, which export proteins before and after they complete folding respectively. SignalP classifies 5 signal peptide classes within the two pathways (SP, LIPO, TAT, TATLIPO, and PILIN). Overall, 13.85% of PETadex sequences were predicted to contain a signal peptide, within which 92.21% of the enzymes have Sec routing and 7.79% have Tat. 90% identity enzyme clusters had on average 93.1% agreement on whether their member sequences are signal peptide-positive, and 98.3% of these positive members shared the same signal peptide class. The gap in agreement within 90% clusters is most likely due to missing N-termini from sequence fragmentation, making SignalP a useful quality check on the database. Alpha/Beta Hydrolases had a below-average percentage of signal peptide-positive sequences (10.6%) but contributed 44% of all predicted secreted ORFs.

### Domain Annotation

Using the complete sequences from the input domains, InterProScan is used to determine
all domains located within the sequence. Then, using PETadex HMMs, HMMER (a tool that
searches through protein sequences to find domains corresponding to inputted HMMs)
searches through the sequences to determine the location of PET-relevant domains in the
sequences.
Now the coordinates of the HMMER results are overlaid on the InterProScan results, and
any domains that hit in both HMMER and InterProScan results are NOT accessory domains,
so they are filtered out. Two resulting datasets are output. One dataset contains all hits
(including overlapping hits) from InterProScan and HMMER and another dataset contains
only accessory domains.

Before launching, we benchmarked the full ESMFold2 model and the lighter ESMFold2-Fast across numbers of diffusion steps and refinement loops. The test sets were 620 centroids stratified by length and origin, 244 experimental PDB structures (204 released after the model's training cutoff) and 110 AlphaFold DB models. On the PDB and AlphaFold DB sets every setting performed the same, with a median TM-score to the experimental structure of 0.989 to 0.990. The difference showed up on short Logan fragments: under 150 aa, Fast lost confidence on 38 of the 65 sequences the reference model was confident about, while above 150 aa it lost none of 441. We therefore used the full model under 150 aa and Fast from 150 aa, both at 32 diffusion steps and 3 refinement loops, the cheapest setting whose results stayed within the run-to-run noise of a 100-step, 20-loop reference.

The run folded 1,793,781 sequences in 16.6 hours on up to 80 GPUs, using 851.6 GPU-hours with no failed folds. Of the 19,430 centroids that already had an AlphaFold DB entry, 27 turned out to point to a related protein rather than the centroid itself, so we folded those too. Across all 1.67 million Fast structures the median mean pLDDT is 92.3, and only 0.6% fall below 70. The short tier, 99.8% of which are Logan fragments, splits in two: 52% fall below 70, mostly around 50, while 24% reach 90 or higher. Every structure can be found from the [PETadex corpus on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/petadex/petadex-corpus-full), whose `structure_path` column gives its location for each 60% centroid.

### Biochemical Properties and Ancestral Reconstruction

Six sequence-derived properties were calculated using Biopython ProtParam for all PETadex ORFs: length, molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), GRAVY hydrophobicity, instability index, and aromaticity. Median values were 308 aa, 34.2 kDa, pI 6.45, GRAVY −0.165, instability 37.5, and aromaticity 0.0885. Compared with a similarly sized random sample of NCBI nr, certain property medians were generally similar (such as 289 aa, GRAVY −0.19), which strengthens PETadex being a database of real functional protein sequences. Additionally, PETadex was far narrower in GRAVY (standard deviation 0.20 vs 0.43), and had fewer comparably short, very long, or highly unstable sequences. This depletion of hydrophobic and extreme sequences is consistent with soluble catalytic domains rather than membrane proteins, supporting PETadex as a promising database for identifying plastic-degrading enzymes. Mature secreted proteins were also more hydrophilic than non-secreted proteins, and catalytic-family median GRAVY correlated strongly with secretion (Spearman ρ = −0.96). Thus the properties serve as a useful filter for sequence selection as well.

Most data on plastic-degrading enzyme activity is locked inside individual published papers, in free-form text and figures that machine learning cannot directly use for activity prediction or enzyme engineering. Reading papers one by one also limits the breadth and depth of research and makes synthesis across papers laborious. To assess and improve the accuracy of the pipeline, human data scrapers are enlisted to manually extract data from a subset of plastic literature using a standardized organizational schema, forming a ground truth for benchmarking LLM results.

### Schema Design and Extraction Protocol

In addition to an automated scraping pipeline, 20+ scrapers spent 450+ hours manually extracting data from 60+ plastic-degradation papers, formatting the data through a standardized interactive HTML schema. The schema captures each paper’s enzymes, experimental variables, setup, and individual activity measurements, then exports the data into structured CSVs. This manually curated dataset provides ground truth for benchmarking LLM-based literature extraction and creates a more filterable resource for future plastic-degradation research.

The schema was created under 3 guiding principles: 1) Standardization in variables, experiment conditions, and units via dropdown selections to support effective data analysis and transferability to LLM guidelines. 2) Structure & Traceability, where autogenerated experiment and enzyme IDs let every extracted data point be backsourced. 3) Guardrailing with built-in error checks to reduce human errors and missing data entries.

Every paper was also independently extracted by two or more scrapers, with 78% of the corresponding raw unreconciled data agreeing within 10%, which supports the schema's strength as an organizer. However, a non-trivial portion of data entries have no matches between pairs of scrapers as a result of scraper(s) overlooking sections of the papers. This showcases how even non-automated pipelines for data extraction are prone to errors, lending strength to the potential benefits of a rigorous LLM-based model.

### Human vs LLM Agreement

Finally, when checking 26 LLM-extracted papers against 26 double-human-verified papers, large discrepancies remain both between the LLM-Human comparisons and Human-Human comparisons. The LLM found 70% of enzymes that either human listed (a second human finds 83%) and 90% of those both humans agreed on. General protein language models are trained mostly on curated databases, while most PETadex sequences come from unassembled metagenomes. We continued pretraining ESM-C 300M [@candido2026esmfold2] on PETadex with masked-language modelling, training only low-rank adapters (LoRA) [@hu2021lora] on all four projections of every transformer block (the attention input and output, and both feed-forward layers). This trains 240 adapter tensors, 14.7 million parameters, with the backbone frozen.

One epoch over all 18.2 million 90%-identity centroids would take 57 hours, so we trained on one randomly drawn member of each of the 1,814,296 60%-identity clusters. A second random seed draws a different member per cluster. Because 48.7% of clusters are singletons, the two draws together hold 2,494,836 distinct sequences, 1.38 times one draw rather than twice.

Training on both draws lowered held-out loss from 1.696 to 1.684 nats (perplexity 5.39). The gain is small but real: about 279 times the difference between two identical runs. Engineering changes cut training time 2.63-fold, and every saved adapter was checked against the live model, with none left untrained. We call the result *PlasticESM*, and its adapters are open on Hugging Face as [🤗 petadex/plasticesm-dualseed](https://huggingface.co/petadex/plasticesm-dualseed).

Before a model can choose what the wet lab tests next, it needs a fair test. We built one on the PETai activity labels (1,137 labelled sequences). Folds are split by connected homology components, so no group of related enzymes spans training and test, and every model is scored against two controls that need no model at all: sequence length and amino-acid composition. Within this benchmark we compared stock ESM-C and PlasticESM, fitting a ridge regression on each model's embeddings for five human-curated activity targets and seven that add LLM-extracted labels, with fold-centred scoring and permutation tests.

The benchmark's first finding concerns evaluation itself. Under the random five-fold splits common in sequence-to-function work, stock ESM-C reaches ρ = 0.72 on turnover number (kcat) and 0.74 on melting temperature, numbers that would look like a working model. Split by homology component, the same embeddings lose 0.49 to 1.01 across the five human-curated targets, while sequence length moves by at most 0.18. Between 90% and 92% of labelled sequences have a homologous relative elsewhere in the set, so a random split almost always scores a model on a relative of something it trained on. Without the component split, we would have reported the inflated numbers.

With leakage removed, the benchmark sets the baseline the next model has to beat. The two controls outscore both embeddings on all twelve target sets: on kcat (227 sequences across 40 components), sequence length reaches ρ = +0.343, against −0.025 for stock ESM-C and −0.217 for PlasticESM. The bottleneck is the labels. Only 53 of the 1,137 labelled sequences (4.7%) appear among the 60%-cluster draws PlasticESM was trained on. Most labels describe engineered variants from the literature rather than natural sequences, and 48% of all labelled sequences sit in a single homology component. The model is being asked to rank point mutants within one family, from labels that barely overlap the population we want to screen. What the next model needs is labels drawn from that population.

### From Round 1 to Round 2

Round 1 will supply the first of those labels. The wet lab will screen the LOBSTER library of 1,744 sequences from the *Is*PETase and LCC clusters, and its results will be added to the literature labels, giving the model measurements on the PETadex sequences it is meant to explore.

Round 2 will then be chosen by the model's uncertainty rather than by its predicted activity. Among the clusters whose environmental metadata looks promising, we will test the ones the model is least sure about, so each new measurement lands where the current labels say least. Each round will be judged against the same component-grouped benchmark and its length and composition controls, so any improvement we report will be one that a random split could not have produced.

## Limitations

**The labels are thin where it matters most.** Only 200 of the activity labels (1.53%) are negatives, and 168 of those come from a single halo screen. Once the split is repaired so that no homology group spans folds, the held-out test fold holds 89 evaluated labels on 16 sequences. One homology component contains 48% of all labelled sequences, so most measurements describe variants of one scaffold. Two shipped targets, relative activity and weight loss, span too few components to be scored at all.

**Our strongest candidate signal is not established.** Structural geometry from predicted structures tracked melting temperature (p = 0.0084), but the same test passes a pure-noise target in 4.5% of null draws. It rests on one family, with nowhere to replicate it.

**The model has not been tested prospectively.** Wet-lab validation so far comes from a single round of assays, and no library has yet been chosen by the model. Round 2 will be the first, selected by model uncertainty, and only its results can show whether that choice beats random sampling.

**Annotation layers carry their own caveats.** Four known profile-rule defects remain unfixed; they touch about 1.5% of profiled BioSamples, and the shift from samples to clusters does not depend on them. The independent temperature check is real but small (ε² = 0.044). Half of BioSamples (50.6%) carry no environment signal, so profiled data leans toward well-described studies. BacDive growth means are unweighted, and the TED novelty cut-offs are provisional.

## Reproducibility and reuse

{/*
  ~200 words. Answers Q4. No figure.
  Open release, documented pipeline, notebooks linked as the primary record
  (github.com/petadex/igem-toronto), generalization to other enzyme families.
*/}

The model and everything it learns from are open on Hugging Face: the PlasticESM adapters ([petadex/plasticesm-dualseed](https://huggingface.co/petadex/plasticesm-dualseed)), the pretraining corpus ([petadex/catalytic-orfs-90pid](https://huggingface.co/datasets/petadex/catalytic-orfs-90pid)) and the activity labels ([petadex/petai-activity-labels](https://huggingface.co/datasets/petadex/petai-activity-labels)), with each label source also published separately. The model card reports the downstream results alongside the weights, so no one reuses the adapters expecting them to predict activity. Each analysis step has a lab notebook as its primary record, with retractions kept in place: label release and split repair in [github.com/petadex/igem-toronto](https://github.com/petadex/igem-toronto), and pretraining and evaluation in [github.com/petadex/petabite](https://github.com/petadex/petabite).

The part we most want others to reuse is the evaluation protocol, not the model. Split by connected homology components rather than at random. Standardise each fold's predictions before pooling them. Use jackknife intervals and permutation tests over components rather than a component bootstrap, which we found excludes zero far too often under the null. And require any learned representation to beat sequence length and composition. These rules are implemented in a small statistics module (`grouped_out_of_fold`, `jackknife_ci`, `permutation_p`) that applies unchanged to any enzyme family with activity labels. Under the conventional random split, we would have reported a correlation of 0.72 for a model that, evaluated correctly, does not yet beat sequence length.
