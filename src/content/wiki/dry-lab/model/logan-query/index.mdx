## Why a second search, and why a smaller query?

To query the Logan contigs I need to hand over a fasta of the sequences I want searched, and the
search costs money in proportion to what is in it. So the query is deliberately narrowed twice over:
a sequence is only included if the HMMs say it has a **complete catalytic domain** — no empty
catalytic residue positions — and only 90% identity centroids of those domains are kept.

That removes two kinds of waste. Sequences whose hit misses part of the catalytic machinery are
off-target for our purposes, and searching accessory domains costs the same as searching catalytic
ones while telling us nothing about plastic degradation.

## Inputs

| Component | Version used | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HMMER | 3.4 (Aug 2023) | `hmmalign`, `hmmsearch` |
| DIAMOND | 2.2.4 | `linclust --id 90 --ext global --comp-based-stats 0` |
| MMseqs2 | Release 18-8cc5c | `easy-linclust`, as an independent check on the clustering |

The inputs are the 53 HMMs from the [previous page](/dry-lab/model/hmm-generation/) and the
significant nr hits from the [nr search](/dry-lab/model/nr-search/). Splitting the significant hits
into per-component fastas first takes the search time from about ten hours down to about one.

## Finding the catalytic domains in nr

Searching the HMMs against the nr homologs produces an annotation row per domain hit, carrying: the
range the HMM hit, the identity of the amino acid found at each catalytic position, and a boolean for
whether the hit is **complete** — a residue present in every catalytic position — or **partial**.

The 53 jobs ran in about 49 minutes and returned **9,468,263 domain-hit rows across 7,562,453 unique
accessions**, before any deduplication or completeness filter. Reducing each accession to its single
lowest-E-value hit leaves **7,562,397 accessions**, of which **7,561,905** are written out as the
PETadex-nr proteins.

## Cutting the domains out

For every hit that survives, the full sequence is looked up in its component's database fasta and
only the amino acids from the HMM start to the HMM end are written. The envelope convention matters
if you are reproducing this, because it is the easiest thing to get wrong:

```python
domain_seq = sequence[hmm_target_start - 1:hmm_target_end]   # 1-based, inclusive
```

Hits annotated as incomplete are dropped before this step, which leaves **6,764,617** complete nr
catalytic domains plus 486 from PETadex-PAZy, spread over 46 component fastas.

## Clustering to the Logan query

Those domains are then clustered at 90% identity with DIAMOND `linclust`, globally rather than
locally, so that a member has to align across its whole length to its centroid. The centroids are the
Logan query: **2,740,458 sequences**.

### Checking DIAMOND against MMseqs2

Clustering with one tool and trusting the count is a good way to overstate how many distinct
sequences you have, so I re-clustered the same input with MMseqs2 and matched the parameters as
closely as the two tools allow:

| MMseqs2 parameter | What it does | DIAMOND equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| `easy-linclust` | linear-time clustering | `linclust` |
| `--min-seq-id 0.9` | a member must share 90% identity with its centroid | `--id 90` |
| `-c 0.9 --cov-mode 0` | the alignment must cover 90% of both centroid and member, mimicking a global alignment | `--ext global` |
| `--alignment-mode 3` | computes the full alignment, including sequence identity | full alignment |
| `--seq-id-mode 0` | identity calculated over the alignment length | identity over the alignment |
| `--comp-bias-corr 0` | turns off composition-bias correction | `--comp-based-stats 0` |

Over the same 6,765,103 input domains, DIAMOND produces **2,740,458 clusters** and MMseqs2
**2,793,566** — a ratio of 0.981, so DIAMOND is making slightly *fewer* clusters, not inflating the
count by splitting. Singletons come out at 1,846,170 against 1,911,582. The one real difference is the
largest cluster: 10,229 sequences under DIAMOND against 5,250 under MMseqs2.

## The Logan search

Rayan Chikhi ran the DIAMONDx search of that query against the Logan contigs and returned the
results: about **10 TB**, or **2.6 TB compressed**, as 100 `.zst` files. I worked through them one at
a time.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Two things this page cannot yet tell you">
The DIAMONDx search parameters were not recorded on our side — version, sensitivity mode, hit limit, E-value cutoff and the Logan release are all outstanding, and need to come from Rayan before this page leaves draft. Separately, the HMM search over the Logan results passed `--total-search-size 1101054423`, the size of **nr**, against a database of 3.2 billion sequences. Every Logan E-value below is therefore on an nr-sized scale, and the 1 × 10⁻⁵ cutoff does not mean what the same cutoff meant in nr.
</Callout>

The raw search returned **5,573,419,026 DIAMONDx rows**, independently counted three ways — by a
bash row count over the compressed files, by the parser, and in the parquet — which all agree.
**2,714,983 of the 2,740,458 query sequences** got at least one hit, and 16,725,463 hits are reported
with an E-value of exactly 0.

## Turning contig hits into ORFs

Logan returns contigs, not proteins, so each hit has to be turned into an open reading frame. For
every row the pipeline cleans the header into TSV, records it in a parquet, then calls the ORF
stop-to-stop on the DNA and finds the frame whose translation exactly contains the reported hit
region. Three cases come out of that:

- **No internal stop** — one ORF.
- **An internal stop splits the hit into two valid ORFs**, both at least 30 aa — both are kept.
- **An internal stop splits the hit but only one side is valid** — the piece under 30 aa is dropped,
  because it is not a protein.

That gives **5,780,630,372 ORF pieces** written to the component fastas: more pieces than there were
hits, because **159,279,814 hits were split** on an internal stop, while **395,454,341 pieces were
dropped** for being under 30 aa. No hit failed to have its frame found. The resulting ORFs run from
the 30 aa floor to a median of 96 aa, with a mean of 150.9 aa.

## Collapsing identical ORFs

nr is 100% non-redundant by construction, so the Logan set is made to match. Within each component,
sequences are deduplicated by exact string match, hits above the 1 × 10⁻⁵ cutoff are dropped, and the
record keeps its lowest-E-value accession first with every duplicate accession listed after it.

**5,780,630,372 ORF pieces collapse to 3,202,954,878 unique sequences**, a ratio of 1.80. The most
repeated single ORF was found **784,796 times**.

## Running 53 HMMs over 3.2 billion ORFs

The deduplicated set is sharded 100 ways per component, so the HMM search is **5,300 jobs** — 53 HMMs
across 100 shards — run 40 at a time. The whole search finished inside 46 minutes, with the reduce
step across sub-components and shards taking another 37. Every job completed; none were killed.

Each sequence with a significant hit is assigned a `petadex_id` of the form `LG{integer}`, and the
annotations carry the same fields as the nr pass: the hit range, the residue identity at each
catalytic position, and whether the hit is complete or partial.

## Extracting the Logan catalytic domains

Extraction follows the nr logic exactly. Every `LG` id is reduced to its single lowest-E-value hit
across every sub-component and domain, its full-length record is written out, and only if that
winning hit is complete is the catalytic domain written to the precluster fasta.

That last condition is worth being explicit about: a sequence whose *best* hit is incomplete is not
clustered even if another of its hits is complete. **291,922,243** sequences pass that best-hit test,
while **297,915,422** have a complete hit somewhere — the 5,993,179 difference is counted separately
as `complete_hit_lost_to_better_incomplete_hit`.

### Complete, truncated, or genuinely partial

**1,442,367,425** sequences have a significant hit but no complete catalytic domain, and it matters a
great deal whether that is because the protein is divergent or because the assembly ran out. A hit
counts as a **truncation** when the missing catalytic positions are explained by the ORF ending:
catalytic sites are ordered by HMM column, and if the missing sites fall before the first present
site the hit has to start within 10 residues of the N-terminus, or after the last present site it has
to end within 10 residues of the C-terminus.

On that rule, **1,190,913,159 are truncations** and **251,454,266 are not** — so the large majority
of incomplete hits are assembly artefacts rather than proteins that genuinely lack the machinery.

That last figure is the honest summary of the whole exercise: completeness is partly a property of
the enzyme family, not only of the assembly. An HMM whose catalytic residues are spread far apart
needs a longer uninterrupted ORF to be satisfied, so its completeness rate will always look worse.

## Limitations

- **The Logan E-values are on an nr-sized scale.** See the caveat above. Everything downstream of the
  1 × 10⁻⁵ cutoff inherits it.
- **The search parameters are not recorded on our side.** Until they come from Rayan Chikhi, this page
  cannot state the sensitivity, the hit limit or the Logan release it describes.
- **Contig-level statistics are not reported here.** The parquet gives a mean of 1.00 hits per contig
  and zero contigs with more than one hit, which cannot be right; the contig key needs checking first.
- **Best-hit extraction discards complete hits.** 5,993,179 sequences have a complete catalytic domain
  that is not clustered, because a better-scoring incomplete hit won the record.
- **The HMMs carry their own limits forward**, including the four sub-components with no model and the
  non-hydrolase regions still inside some envelopes.

## What is still to come

The Logan catalytic domains are **not yet clustered**. The precluster count above stands in for what
will eventually be the Logan 90% identity centroids, so the corpus is not final and the numbers on
this page are the state of a draft notebook rather than a finished stage. Clustering across the nr and
Logan domains together is the next step.

## Reproducing

```bash
# 1. search the HMMs against the nr homologs, per component, and annotate
#    hit ranges, catalytic residue identities and completeness
./hmm_search.sh --hmms petadex_hmms/ --targets per_component/ --evalue 1e-5

# 2. extract complete catalytic domains, then cluster at 90% identity, globally
diamond linclust -d nr_catalytic_domains.fastaa -o clusters.tsv \
  --id 90 --ext global --comp-based-stats 0

# 3. after the Logan search returns, parse each .pro.zst into ORFs, dedup at
#    100% identity, shard, then search the same HMMs over the shards
./logan_hmm_search.sh --shards per_component_shards/ --jobs 40 \
  --total-search-size 1101054423   # nr-sized Z — see the caveat above
```
