## Why build our own HMMs?

The nr search returns sequences that are predicted to be homologous to plastic-degrading enzymes,
but we cannot confidently say they contain the domains needed to catalyse the degradation of plastic
polymers. To make that claim we need HMMs seeded from the catalytic domains of the verified enzymes
themselves.

I realised that last time I made these HMMs, I had no way of figuring out whether they were
overfitting. So this time the HMMs are built on hits below E-10 and judged on the hold-out band
between E-10 and E-5. Restricting what can enter an HMM to the stronger hits forces it to represent
the query sequences rather than the whole database.

The HMMs get used six ways:

1. Extracting only the catalytic domain for the Logan search, so we do not pay to search accessory
   domains.
2. Annotating catalytic domains for each sequence on the website.
3. Annotating catalytic residues.
4. Annotating secondary residues — important but non-catalytic.
5. Telling us which Logan sequences are non-fragmented, since an assembly is inherently fragmented
   and a full HMM hit is the evidence of completeness.
6. Finding motifs in the HMM logos, which may inform mechanistic analysis.

That fifth use needs care, because a half-covered catalytic domain has three very different causes:

- **A.** Only half the domain is homologous enough to hit the HMM — a lower-quality homolog.
- **B.** There is a large insertion between the two halves, so only half is hit — a higher-quality
  homolog with a solvable problem.
- **C.** The edge of the hit is the true edge of the domain — the domain really is fragmented.

Telling A, B and C apart requires confidence that the HMMs themselves are good, which means the seed
alignments have to be good. That is what this page is about.

## Annotating the catalytic residues

To decide what a good alignment even looks like, I went through each CATH domain and learned as much
about it as I could: the mechanism, and which residues matter. Those annotations are what let me
infer the catalytic mechanism of each component and build a reference sequence for each one. The
annotations were made on a representative per CATH domain and then propagated to a representative
for each sub-component.

## Seeding the alignments with close homologs

A problem I keep hitting is that HMMs built from very few sequences — under ten — have terrible seed
alignments. With that few sequences there is no way to tell a conserved column from an accident.

  alt="Alignment viewer showing the C034 alignment built from fewer than ten PETadex-PAZy sequences, with no clearly conserved columns and large gapped regions."
  caption={<><strong>Figure 1.</strong> The C034 alignment built from PETadex-PAZy sequences alone. With this few sequences there is nothing to distinguish a conserved column from an accident, which is why the seed alignments were padded with close homologs first.</>}
  width="100%"
/>

So the seeds are padded with close nr homologs, taken at **DIAMOND E < 1.0 × 10⁻⁵⁰** to keep them
very homologous to PETadex-PAZy. That still produces thousands of highly redundant sequences per
component, so they are clustered at an identity chosen from the component's size:

| Sequences in component | Clustered at |
|---|---|
| 50,000+ | 30% identity |
| 10,000+ | 50% identity |
| 100+ | 70% identity |
| 1+ | 90% identity |

Tuning the clustering to the sequence count gives a subset small enough to align without becoming
gappy, but large enough to show which columns are the consensus columns. The target is roughly
100–1,000 sequences per alignment.

The first alignment pass uses MUSCLE's Super5 algorithm, which trades accuracy for speed. That is
fine here, because these sequences are going to be cleaned and re-aligned anyway.

### Splitting components into sub-components

Looking at the alignments at the catalytic residue columns, eleven sub-components needed to be split
out of their parent: five from C001 (PL121, PL69, PL214, PL120, PL119), one from C006 (PL88), one
from C008 (PL46), and four from C014 (PL453/PL164/PL166/PL187, PL114, PL74, PL115).

### Dropping sequences that miss the catalytic columns

Every sequence entering an alignment has to carry the canonical catalytic residues of its CATH
domain. This keeps functional diversity inside an HMM from getting too wide, so the seed alignments
hold only sequences that align properly and have the right residues.

### Trimming the accessory domains by hand

With smallish alignments per component, I could go through them and clean them up. The main thing I
removed was accessory domains. These are why each alignment took so long to run — overnight — since
they forced alignments upwards of **50,000 columns** long to accommodate unalignable accessory
regions.

The rule I used: find the first column that is highly conserved for a residue — no hard cutoff, but
generally above 50% conservation and above 50% of maximum quality — and remove everything to its
left. Then do the same with the last highly conserved column and remove everything to its right.
Having the first and last column of the alignment be highly conserved is what keeps the HMM on the
catalytic domain.

### Re-aligning with PPP

With accessory domains gone, the alignments were rebuilt with MUSCLE's PPP algorithm instead of
Super5, which improves their quality. C016 was taking far too long under PPP, so it stayed on Super5.

### Four sub-components with no HMM

Four sub-components were dropped rather than modelled:

- **C001-5** and **C008-1** have about one nr homolog each, and building an HMM off one sequence is
  not worthwhile. On investigation, PL119 (the C001-5 representative) is actually a fragment that
  somehow had activity — my guess is the 6×His tag helped — and for PL46 I have no idea what the
  mechanism is.
- **C001-4** and **C014-4** were suspiciously missing catalytic residues. C001-4 turns out to be a
  fragment, and for C014-4 I cannot work out what the mechanism could be. Without a mechanism there
  is no way to measure HMM quality, so neither got one.

All four keep their annotations in the reference sheet.

## The iterative build

The build pipeline is comparable to JackHMMER, with one addition: a sequence can only enter the next
iteration if it carries the canonical catalytic residues.

1. `hmmbuild` the HMM from the input MSA.
2. `hmmalign` the HMM to the reference sequence, to transfer the catalytic site annotations.
3. `hmmsearch` the HMM against its component's fasta.
4. Parse the output into an alignment of only those domain hits that contain every catalytic residue
   — exact matches to the residue identity — and fall below the E-value cutoff. If a sequence has
   two catalytic domains that both pass, both are added.
5. Annotate columns above the coverage threshold as match states and the rest as insertions.
6. Seed the resulting MSA into the next iteration and repeat.

## Training and validation sets

The sets are the significant nr hits plus the PETadex-PAZy proteins, which go into training only:

| Set | Definition | Sequences |
|---|---|---|
| Training | nr hits below E-10, plus the PETadex-PAZy proteins | 5,997,120 |
| Validation | nr hits between E-10 and E-5 | 1,828,480 |

Those sum to 7,825,600, which is the 7,825,107 significant nr sequences plus the 493 PETadex-PAZy
proteins. Both sets are split per component, which cuts the pipeline's run time substantially.

## Choosing symfrac, E-value and iteration count

I wanted to test different `--symfrac` values, E-value cutoffs and iteration counts, so the sweep ran
only on components whose `hmmsearch` takes about three seconds — C001, C013, C041 and C045 — rather
than overnight on everything.

```bash
COMBOS=("0.5 1e-5" "0.5 1e-10" "0.5 1e-15"
        "0.7 1e-5" "0.7 1e-10" "0.7 1e-15"
        "0.9 1e-5" "0.9 1e-10" "0.9 1e-15")
ITERATIONS="1_plasticome.v2,2_unrefined_petadex,3_refined_petadex,4_overkill,5_overkill_overkill"
NCBI_SIZE=1101054423   # passed to hmmsearch as --total-search-size
```

Five iterations because JackHMMER's maximum is five, so that is the number worth testing to.

### Does the HMM stay inside the catalytic domain?

It is hard to tell how good an HMM is without looking at where it hits on a protein. Making that
automatic meant taking as many structures of the nr hits as possible and finding their domain
boundaries, then measuring what portion of the catalytic domain the HMM covers. The structure
mapping holds **575,770 rows** (126 skipped for a malformed component id); up to 1,000 structures per
component were sampled, giving **36,322 structures**, of which **36,321 were cut** successfully by
AFRAGMENTER — the one failure was a 404 from AlphaFold DB.

One thing the analysis has to allow for: catalytic domains are often cut at lids or caps, because
those regions are flexible. A hit spanning a lid has to be compared against the whole catalytic
domain, not against half of one.

Coverage is about 90% of the domain for most components. The exceptions: C001, C012, C026, C028 and
C039 average about 80%, C014 and C027 about 70%, and C015 about 50%. Out-of-domain coverage averages
10% or less almost everywhere, with C006 around 25% — bimodal near 0 and 50 — and C008 around 30%.
There are stragglers that hit far outside the catalytic domain; C014's apparent 1400% is an
AFRAGMENTER artefact, where a cut site landed about five residues inside the catalytic domain and
that five-residue insert became the thing being measured against. I could fix that by tuning
AFRAGMENTER, but I do not think it is worth the time.

### The parameters we settled on

**symfrac.** There is no significant difference between 0.7 and 0.9: both give about the same number
of catalytically complete hits and total hits, and hit about the same regions. The one real
difference is that 0.9 fails for C015 while 0.7 works, so **0.7** it is.

**Iterations.** Little separates iterations 3 through 5, but 5 has slightly negative effects —
reducing hits, and the hit region becoming less accurate — and 3 does not plateau. So **4**, as the
middle of the two, minimising both problems.

The final HMMs are the symfrac 0.7, E 1e-10, iteration 4 builds, and the file name is the parameter
record:

```bash
cp symfrac0.7_evalue1e-10__4_overkill__plasticome.component_${id}.hmm \
   petadex.v260701-${id}.hmm
```

## Looking at the hits on the structures

I also wanted to look at the hit regions on the structures by hand, to see how much of each domain is
really covered and whether the catalytic residue annotations are right. For each final HMM the script
samples up to four complete hits, three fragment hits missing a catalytic position, and three
structures with no hit at all. A structure is only picked for the HMM that gives it its lowest
E-value within its component.

The annotation style, for reading those images:

- the protein is a white cartoon
- each HMM domain hit gets its own colour in sequence order
- catalytic residues are hot pink sticks, labelled with the residue actually found there, with a
  yellow outline where it is not the expected amino acid
- residues within 5 Å of a catalytic residue are thin, element-coloured sticks

Three findings from that pass are worth keeping on the record:

- **C010, C017, C019 and C030** have non-hydrolase regions inside the HMM envelope. They are
  genuinely part of the protein and arguably the same domain, but I believe they have another
  function — a transmembrane domain — so they should come out of the input MSAs.
- **C001-2 and C014-2** have no AlphaFold DB structures to look at, so this validation was never run
  on them. They have very few nr hits, which makes those two HMMs hard to evaluate at all.
- **C001-1's histidine looks to be in the wrong position.** The actual protein does have a histidine
  there, but all of its homologs have it somewhere else. This protein is one of the strange Erickson
  fragments. I am keeping the annotation, in the hope it tells us something about that protein, but
  otherwise this one should be taken with a grain of salt.

Finally, I wanted to see how similar the finished HMMs are, so the 53 of them are laid out as a
minimum spanning tree whose edges are the probability of homology given by HHalign. In theory each
component should be its own cluster; in practice that is only partly true.

## Limitations

- **Four sub-components have no HMM** (C001-4, C001-5, C008-1, C014-4), so sequences belonging to
  them are only ever caught by their parent component's model.
- **C001-2 and C014-2 were never structurally validated**, for want of AlphaFold DB structures.
- **C015 is the weakest model**, covering about half its catalytic domain on average, and it is also
  the component that forced symfrac 0.7 over 0.9.
- **The AFRAGMENTER boundaries are predictions, not truth.** C014's 1400% out-of-domain figure is a
  cut-site artefact rather than an HMM failure, and the same class of error will inflate other
  out-of-domain numbers to a smaller degree.
- **The non-hydrolase regions in C010, C017, C019 and C030 are still inside the envelope** of the
  HMMs as built here.

## Reproducing

```bash
# per component: build, transfer catalytic annotations, search, re-seed, four times
./iterative_hmmbuild.sh \
  --msa plasticome.component_${id}.afa \
  --targets training/${id}.fastaa \
  --symfrac 0.7 --evalue 1e-10 --iterations 4 \
  --total-search-size 1101054423

# the iteration-4 build is the shipped model
cp symfrac0.7_evalue1e-10__4_overkill__plasticome.component_${id}.hmm \
   petadex_hmms/petadex.v260701-${id}.hmm
```
