## Why search nr?

The goal of this step is to retrieve as many homologs of plastic-degrading enzymes as possible from
the NCBI non-redundant protein database. When someone studies a protein and obtains its amino acid
sequence, they deposit that sequence with the National Center for Biotechnology Information. NCBI
adds it to the nr database, and if the same amino acid sequence has already been deposited it is not
added again — hence non-redundant. The new accession and its metadata are appended to the existing
record instead.

We want every homolog of PETadex-PAZy that nr holds, because that set becomes the seed query against
Logan: the homologs of homologs are where the scale comes from. Whatever nr returns still has to be
quality controlled with HMMs afterwards, to check that the hit lands on the catalytic domain rather
than somewhere else in the protein. That is the [next page](/dry-lab/model/hmm-generation/).

One consequence is worth stating up front. PETadex-PAZy sequences are themselves in nr, so the
search finds them. Every hit whose accession or exact amino acid sequence matches a PETadex-PAZy
entry is removed from the nr set, which keeps the two datasets non-redundant with respect to each
other. The whole corpus is therefore clustered at 100% identity by construction: a protein here
means a 100% identity centroid.

## Inputs

| Component | Version used | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NCBI nr | ncbi-blast-2.17.0+ | Already downloaded and indexed on the server, then converted to a DIAMOND database |
| DIAMOND | 2.2.4.184 | `blastp`, `--very-sensitive` |

The query is the **411** PETadex-PAZy 90% identity centroids produced by
[Component Assignment](/dry-lab/model/component-assignment/). 410 of them came back with a
significant hit; the one that did not is covered [below](#hits-per-query-sequence).

## The search

The flags were chosen for three reasons:

- `--outfmt 6` gives the comprehensive tabular output that the parser downstream expects.
- `--very-sensitive` searches below 40% identity, into the twilight zone where most remote homologs
  of our seeds sit.
- `-k0` allows unlimited hits per query, so a query that has a million homologs is not truncated.

```bash
diamond blastp -d ncbi-blast_nr-2.17.0+.dmnd -q petadex-pazy.v260701.90pid-centroids.fastaa \
  --very-sensitive -k0 -b140 -c1 --threads 120 \
  --outfmt 6 qseqid sseqid pident length mismatch gapopen qstart qend sstart send evalue bitscore
```

## From 42 million hits to 7.8 million sequences

The search returned **42,053,081 hits**. Many query sequences hit the same target, and one amino acid
sequence can carry several GenBank accessions, so the parser keeps the lowest E-value hit per
non-redundant sequence. That leaves **8,850,583 unique amino acid sequences** before any E-value
cutoff is applied.

Applying the cutoff of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ gives **7,825,107 amino acid sequences**, spanning **13,209,543
unique GenBank accessions**.

### Removing PETadex-PAZy from its own results

Every sequence in the significant-hits file is cross-checked against PETadex-PAZy on two keys: the
accession and the exact amino acid sequence. Matches are removed from the nr set, and the matching
accession is recorded in the header of the PETadex-PAZy sequence it matched, so the provenance is
not lost.

### The 36 accessions that did not come back

36 PETadex-PAZy sequences with GenBank accessions were not retrieved by the search. I went through
every one of them, and they fall into three groups:

| Why | Count | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Record suppressed at NCBI, so it is no longer in nr | 29 | `PL136 / WP_083947829.1` |
| MGnify accession, never in GenBank | 6 | `PL13 / MGYP000532440779` |
| Sequence too short for the E-value to clear the cutoff | 1 | `PL56 / P80008.1` |

I can explain why every one of them is absent, so this step is fine.

### Hits per component

Most components have somewhere between 10,000 and 100,000 homologs in nr, so the hits are fairly
spread out. A few components hit very high:

- **C16**, cytochrome homologs. This makes a great deal of sense: cytochromes are very well studied
  across all kingdoms of life, including in humans. They are oxygenases and the main enzymes of drug
  metabolism, so they have evolved to break down almost anything.
- **C3**, amidases, which break N–C bonds. We do not have many amidase components, so it makes sense
  that this one takes a large share.
- **C34**, a group of α/β hydrolases, possibly carboxylesterases. I can't tell you why there are so
  many of these.
- **C14**, another set of α/β hydrolases, perhaps esterases. Also can't tell you why there are so many.

Overall about **75% of the database is attributable to just 8 of the 46 components**.

At the other end are four singletons: **C39** (a PLA depolymerase), **C33** (an archaeal PETase),
**C23** (an unknown carboxylesterase) and **C27** (a PLA depolymerase from the same paper as C39).
It would be good to see how many more hits Logan can find for these understudied ones.

C1 is the IsPETase homologs, including LCC, and it accounts for a lot of hits — useful if we want to
deep-dive that component later.

### Hits per query sequence

Some query sequences dominate their component, and some dominate the whole dataset. Just **three
sequences account for 25%** of the DIAMOND output:

- **PL175**, a cytochrome P450 singleton cluster
- **PL128**, a DD-peptidase singleton cluster
- **PL109**, a urethanase amidase, not a singleton

The top two are singletons, so if there were more sequences in those components the hits would
probably be split across the component the way they are in C1. Just **11 sequences account for 50%**
of the hits, so the dataset is less evenly distributed than I would have liked.

One query sequence, **PL411**, is credited with no hits at all. Its top BLAST hits are either
PETadex-PAZy sequences or sequences that hit PETadex-PAZy. In the raw output PL411 does have hits —
every one of them simply scored better against another query, so under best-hit attribution it is
never credited with them.

Here is what the characterised seeds pulled in:

The well-studied α/β hydrolases have relatively few hits of their own, because their hits are being

## Did doubling PETadex-PAZy help?

PETadex-PAZy roughly doubled between v1 and v2, so the question is whether the larger query actually
reaches new sequence space or just resamples the same space more heavily.

| Statistic | v1 | v2 |
|---|---|---|
| Redundant hits | 30,872,783 | 42,053,081 |
| Non-redundant hits | 7,271,879 | 8,850,583 |
| Significant hits | 6,295,887 | 7,825,107 |

The v2-only hits are the interesting half, and they are new sequence space rather than more of the
same:

- **PL175 / P08684.4**, 1,299,987 hits. Cytochrome P450: new component, new sequence space.
- **PL174 / P00761.1**, 355,608 hits. Chymotrypsin. The closest centroid in the search is 34%
  identical, and this is a mammalian trypsin, so it is likely to hit a great deal.
- **PL378 / WDT94443**, 294,874 hits. Epoxide hydrolase; the closest centroid is almost unalignable.
- **PL388 / 1ETH_A**, 186,822 hits. Porcine lipase, brand new to PAZy and to PETadex-PAZy.
- **PL244 / WP_067035690.1**, 127,654 hits. This one is picking up a galactose-binding domain.

Almost all of the v1-only losses trace to four sequences whose accessory domains were trimmed in v2:

- **MBO9320024.1**, 292,146 hits. The Erickson 214 protein. In v2 its 90% identity centroid (PL91,
  59,526 hits) was searched instead, and the v1 sequence carried an accessory domain the centroid
  does not, which accounts for the excess.
- **WP_085690612.1**, 60,525 hits. MG8, whose amino acid sequence changed substantially between v1
  and v2 along with its source paper, apparently from a PAZy update. InterProScan calls the v1
  sequence an acyl-protein thioesterase or carboxylesterase-like protein and the v2 sequence a
  PETase, so the v2 sequence is the one to trust and the v1 hits were picking up the wrong thing.
- **MCS7037955.1**, 31,165 hits. Erickson 301, whose v1 sequence has a C-terminal accessory domain
  that v2 trims.
- **MDT7859148.1**, 17,352 hits. Erickson 307, 70 amino acids longer in v1.

So the doubling bought real expansion, and the losses are cases where the trimmed sequence is the
more honest query.

## Limitations

- **Best-hit attribution hides a query's reach.** A sequence is credited only to its best-scoring
  query, which is why PL411 appears to have no hits at all. Per-query counts are therefore a
  statement about attribution, not about homology.
- **The dataset is uneven.** 8 components hold about 75% of it, and 11 sequences hold 50%. Anything
  trained on this corpus has to account for that skew rather than assume breadth.
- **Suppressed and MGnify-only records are unreachable.** 29 PETadex-PAZy accessions no longer exist
  in nr and 6 were never in GenBank, so their sequences cannot contribute homologs here.
- **No domain-level quality control yet.** Everything on this page is sequence-level similarity. A
  hit that aligns to the wrong part of the protein is still a hit at this stage; the HMMs on the
  next page are what filter for the catalytic domain.

## Reproducing

```bash
# 1. convert the indexed BLAST nr database to a DIAMOND database
diamond prepdb -d ncbi-blast_nr-2.17.0+

# 2. search the PETadex-PAZy centroids against it
diamond blastp -d ncbi-blast_nr-2.17.0+.dmnd -q petadex-pazy.v260701.90pid-centroids.fastaa \
  --very-sensitive -k0 -b140 -c1 --threads 120 \
  --outfmt 6 qseqid sseqid pident length mismatch gapopen qstart qend sstart send evalue bitscore \
  > petadex-nr.v260701.diamond.tsv

# 3. keep the lowest-E-value hit per non-redundant sequence, cut at 1e-5,
#    then remove every hit that matches PETadex-PAZy by accession or exact sequence
```
