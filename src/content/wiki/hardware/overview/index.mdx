## Abstract: A Bioreactor Beyond Plastics

PET plastic pollution is a century-old problem, and plastic-degrading enzymes (PETases) offer a promising solution. Yet, finding the enzyme that works best under a given set of conditions, for example, temperature or pH, remains a central challenge. The dry lab team has assembled a database of millions of PET-degrading enzymes and trained a model on it to rank which candidates are most worth testing. The wet lab team then sends these candidates through novel high throughput assays to validate the model’s predictions. However, these assays are limited to one condition at a time. Full characterization of an enzyme’s behaviour or fitness landscape requires tests across varying temperatures, pH values, and media. This ground-truth data can provide valuable feedback to the dry lab, allowing them to improve their models over time.

So how do we complete the loop? This year, iGEM Toronto’s hardware team is proud to introduce a new bioreactor that is accessible for wet lab researchers, both in terms of its user interface and its assembly, that can work in parallel batches of up to 96 bioreactors, and contains sensors and actuators that provide the basic functionalities including temperature regulation, OD600 readings, and stirring, all for a total price less than $250 (about $204 CAD per bioreactor). Stakeholder interviews with wet lab members, engineering experts, and even CEOs informed our user interface, which connects to bioreactors using an intuitive USB serial connection. Plug it directly into your laptop and a wall socket, and the bioreactor is ready for usage.

The most recent design supports three peristaltic pumps: two inlet pumps and one outlet pump for optical density (OD) control, extracting fluid, and future pH control. It also uses one heater, to reach a peak temperature of approximately 60 degrees Celsius, and maintains temperature to within one degree of an accepted thermometer value, holding 37 °C within ±0.3 °C during testing. The stirring is completed by a stir bar and a PC fan with speed control down to ~200 RPM. The overall design is powered by a USB-C charger that supports up to 7 bioreactors, and the current USB hub can house up to 7 bioreactors per laptop USB port. In our final experiment, the bioreactor successfully grew E. coli over 4 hours, confirmed by spectrophotometer readings. iGEM hardware’s bioreactor makes testing the plastic-degrading activity of PETases possible, but its features make it an accessible, user-friendly, and highly efficient solution for anyone hoping to automate their assays under many conditions.

<h3 id="final-temperature">Temperature</h3>

The temperature subsystem maintains the temperature in the bioreactor to ±1oC up to 60oC, supporting the growth of bacteria whose ideal environments are hotter than ambient. This is achieved through custom PID-based logic controlling a hardware feedback loop: temperature probe -> microcontroller -> relay -> heater.

<h3 id="final-power">Power Supply</h3>

A USB-C based power delivery system provides stable 5V 20W power in a user-friendly form factor. Up to 7 bioreactors can share a single charging block.

<h3 id="final-pumps">Peristaltic Pumps</h3>

The peristaltic pump subsystem allows for constant flow of fluid into and out of the bioreactor, providing a sterile environment for pH control. The system architecture supports two inlet pumps and one outlet pump, with the subsystem fully functioning for one pump prototype. The DC motor in the pump subsystem works to move fluid by having rollers that squeeze parts of the pump at a time, allowing fluids to be pushed through. The pumps can run with varying power levels using PWM control with an Arduino nano, transistor, and flyback diode, allowing for precise dosing. The pumps have a snap-fit mechanism that allows the tubing to be easily accessible for autoclaving purposes.

<h3 id="final-od">OD</h3>

Optical density (OD) is a measurement of how much light is scattered or absorbed by cells in a liquid culture. As the OD changes, it reflects the growth of a bacterial culture, which we can quantify as a scalar value of the original substance (a normalized value, such as 1.5x of the original starting concentration) or a standardized value comparable to other devices (OD600 utilized in spectrophotometers).

The configuration requires a red LED and 2 OD sensors–one to account for environmental drift (reference sensor) and the other to measure how much light passes through the vial (collecting sensor).

<h3 id="final-data">Data and controls</h3>

The software subsystem supports two-way communication between the bioreactor (via Arduino) and laptop, integrating live monitoring, data visualization, and controls into a user-friendly interface. The main dashboard displays key live readings and controls, while the status tab provides an overview of connected ports, corresponding bioreactors, and their operating conditions. The OD Calibration tab simplifies calibration through a three-button sequence with live graphical feedback, while the temperature and spectrophotometer tabs display live readings and a time series graph of live readings to help users monitor experimental conditions over time. Furthermore, the dashboard can be run locally with very few commands, as it is contained in a single Python file. This subsystem reduces the need for separate monitoring tools for each variable, simplifies ease of setup, and supports efficient control of bioreactors.

Furthermore, building on V2, a status tab was added to showcase the status of numerous bioreactors, as shown. When implementing the corresponding code, it was noted that some controls were being sent to all bioreactors instead of simply just the select one. Thus, minor edits were made to correct this such that controls would be sent only to the selected bioreactor.

The code for the final version of the Data + Controls subsystem can be found [here](https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/toronto/hardware/-/blob/main/Data%20and%20Controls/Vfinal.py).

## Final Results

We ran two separate tests on our bioreactor with one main goal: growing E. coli! In both experiments, we ran the bioreactor systems over several hours. This meant heating the bioreactor to an appropriate temperature, stirring the media inside, and testing OD readings. The expected outcome was to see optical density increase over time as the E. coli grew. In the first experiment, the OD600, as measured via an external spectrophotometer, did not change, indicating zero bacterial growth. We identified the reasons behind that: limited testing time and nonfunctional stirring. The next day, we re-tested the bioreactor over 4 hours and were able to see E. coli growth. These results showcase the biocompatibility of our bioreactor with E. coli. More importantly, they demonstrate the potential of our bioreactor to be used with genetically-engineered E. coli for PETases as directed by the dry lab and wet lab subteams.

### Experiment 0:

We tested the final design without bacteria to see if components and the UI worked as planned. The test revealed that the UI graphs and sliders were not displaying the OD readouts and controlling the temperature setpoints, respectively. Wiring errors were also identified and addressed. These bugs were addressed before our first experiment with bacteria.

### Experiment 1:

<Figure
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6187/wiki/hardware-notebook/overview/finalresult/4.avif"

In our first test with bacteria, we attempted to measure the growth of a 20 mL E. coli liquid culture incubated overnight for 1.5 hours at 37°C. At this point, the stirring feature was non-functional as the magnets had not been added to the new fan component. The expectation was for the OD to drop initially as the E. coli from the fresh culture would gradually settle at the bottom of the 40 mL vial. 1.5 hours was not expected to be enough to see bacteria growth. After the experiment, all components in contact with E. coli were sprayed with alcohol and prepared for a second experiment the following day.

In less than 20 minutes, the bioreactor reached its target temperature (37°C +/- 0.1°C) from room temperature (~22 °C). Throughout the duration of the experiment, the temperature remained within +/-0.3°C of the target at all times. With the uncertainty of the temperature sensor being +/-0.5°C, the temperature stability met our heating requirements.

<Figure
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6187/wiki/hardware-notebook/overview/finalresult/5.avif"
  alt="5"
  caption="Figure 5: Bioreactor setup for both experiments. Arduino connected to laptop via USB serial connection for data transfer and perf board powered via wall socket. UI controls were displayed on the laptop."
/>

Both the initial and final OD600, as measured by a spectrophotometer, were 0.438. The constant OD reading meant that the time window of 1.5 hours was too short to accrue meaningful data on bacterial growth, as expected initially. This insight led to a longer second experiment the following day. The magnitude of OD readings was incorrect and hovered around zero due to an incorrect calibration procedure at the beginning. This was also corrected for Experiment 2. After removing the vial, mixing, and returning the solution to the bioreactor, however, the OD reading from the bioreactor was 0.454, more comparable to that of the device. The similarity of this reading to that of the spectrophotometer was likely a coincidence rather than an indicator of its accuracy because the value changed significantly after re-adding the vial.

Moreover, the rate of change of the OD readings was correct and demonstrated a slight dip as the E. coli settled and a slight increase toward the end of the experiment. The larger spikes in OD were attributed to bumping the table, which changed the configuration of the LED slightly, disrupting the OD readings. For Test #3, the OD sensor shall be held in place more securely to prevent these signal errors. Filters were also considered to reduce statistical noise in the OD signal.

The fan and pumps were not explicitly tested with the E. coli; however, the fan and pump controls were shown to function at earlier steps of the design process.

#### Summary of Lessons:

- Make the OD calibration process easier for the user to understand
- Jumper wires were exposed and constantly falling out of their ports
- Bioreactor was shaking, leading to the LED moving and disrupting the OD values
- Some light was entering the interior of the vial (minor effect)
- Lack of stirring was more critical than expected initially. Without stirring, OD measurements cannot be correctly calibrated as the E. coli pools at the bottom. Furthermore, since E. coli is not being exposed to the oxygen and nutrient-rich media, the E. coli does not grow as quickly as expected.

### Experiment 2:

With addition of a sturdy base and OD sensors. The initial OD was measured to be 0.153. A new magnet was added to the fan to rotate the stir bar. We found that mixing was challenging for small stir bars and at high speeds (2000+ RPM); only with medium-sized stir bars and slower speeds was the stir bar mixing adequate to create a vortex. After 3 hours outside the incubator and 4 hours in the bioreactor, the OD of a 500 uL aliquot of the media diluted with 500 uL of raw LB was measured via spectrophotometry. Due to the dilution factor of 0.5, two OD readings were taken, 1.25 and 1.166, demonstrating that the bacteria did grow in the bioreactor. Goal achieved!

Only at low RPMs (&lt;2000) did the magnets successfully spin the stir bar. This time, we believe the stirring allowed the E. coli to grow quicker leading to the dramatic increase in OD.

Thus, the change in OD for a given run of the bioreactor was slightly accurate, however, the absolute OD values were entirely incorrect and OD values between runs of the bioreactor were incompatible with each other due to slight changes in the arrangement of LEDs and other components.

Nevertheless, the growth of E. coli via the ground-truth spectrophotometer readings demonstrates our bioreactor can facilitate the environmental conditions for bacterial growth. Each consecutive experiment highlighted an area of growth that was addressed in the following experiment. For instance, the UI functionality errors to the stirring magnets to, finally, the OD600 readings. We hope to continue moving our design forward, one experiment at a time, to complete a bioreactor that is ready to measure the activity of PETases to wet lab and dry lab’s desires.

## Next Steps

Experiment 2 demonstrated that the OD600 readings still needed significant work. The next steps for our bioreactor were listed in order of priority. These steps will be taken in future design iterations:

1. Make OD reliable. Set up the calibration as done with the yeast with at least 5 known concentrations and secure the LED and sensors more effectively. Then, validate the nOD values and see how well they match the ground-truth spectrophotometer.
2. Add or repurpose existing OD sensors to read at multiple wavelengths. This will be useful to read dye release for PET degradation.
3. Improve wire visibility with improved CAD shells.
4. Upgrade perfboard to support up to 7 bioreactors on one laptop and one wall socket.
