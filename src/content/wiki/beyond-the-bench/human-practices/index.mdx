Plastic pollution does not stop at borders and neither should the solution. A technology that works only in a well-funded Canadian treatment plant solves a narrow slice of the problem for the communities that can already afford infrastructure. We built PetaBite around that constraint. From the start, we set a moonshot goal of building a workflow that produces highly efficient plastic-degrading enzymes for any set of operating conditions, rather than optimizing a single enzyme for a single environment.

So we pursued that goal on two fronts: listening to the people our technology would affect and opening synthetic biology to people who have never had access to it.

To hold ourselves to that standard, we produced two formal reports.

### Regulatory Oversight

The Regulatory Oversight Framework maps what it would actually take to deploy a biological solution responsibly. It covers our open science obligations under Canada's Roadmap for Open Science and the FAIR principles, benefit-sharing duties under the Nagoya Protocol for enzyme sequences drawn from biodiversity-rich nations, dual-use research of concern, and the AI governance frameworks that apply to PETadex, including PIPEDA, the Directive on Automated Decision-Making, and the EU AI Act.

On the biosafety side, the regulatory framework examines our project under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act and includes country‑specific profiles for Pakistan, India, Nigeria, Indonesia, and Canadian Indigenous communities, developed from primary stakeholder interviews. These profiles evaluate the ethics of deploying a Global North technology in regions disproportionately affected by plastic pollution, addressing waste colonialism, ethics dumping, and the extent to which open access enables genuine usability in areas with limited infrastructure and technical capacity.

### Environmental and Sustainability Considerations

The Sustainability Report evaluates whether our solution mitigates existing problems without creating new ones. It measures our environmental footprint across the energy demands of the AI pipeline, enzyme synthesis and production, hardware use, biological and chemical waste streams, and water consumption. It also includes carbon emission calculations for our Dry Lab, Wet Lab, and Hardware operations.

Beyond environmental metrics, the report addresses microplastic fate, enzyme containment, and integration within a circular economy. On the social side, it examines cost and accessibility, public perception of engineered enzymes and AI‑driven databases across cultural and regional contexts, and outlines an ethical deployment framework specifying the evidence required before release. We close with recommendations for the field and directions for future research.

### Education

On the education side, we hosted <strong>three conferences</strong>, mentored <strong>two high school teams</strong> in synthetic biology, ran sustained exchanges with <strong>four universities abroad</strong>, and carried out <strong>eight campus collaborations</strong> across the University of Toronto that reached over <strong>800</strong> students and community members cumulatively.

### Advancing the Plastic-Degrading Enzyme Research Landscape

Our Dry Lab team developed the **PETadex (petadex.net)**, the largest open-source database of plastic-degrading enzymes. The PETadex democratizes access to enzyme discovery by providing researchers with a curated dataset of PETases and related enzymes, **removing many of the computational and financial barriers associated with large-scale sequence analysis.**

As a result, teams around the world can accelerate PETase research, engineering, and characterization using a shared, accessible resource.

## Commitment to Inclusivity

Solving a global challenge requires input from the people who will be most affected by the solution. We embedded this principle throughout our project by building it into our recruitment practices, laboratory culture, and engagement with advisors, stakeholders, and community members.

<Dropdown title="Holistic stakeholder engagement">

We engaged every group our technology affects. Plant operators described how facilities function in practice. Regulators at Environment and Climate Change Canada and the U.S. EPA mapped the approval pathways we must navigate. Community organizations such as the U of T Trash Team challenged our framing. Indigenous knowledge holders reframed plastic pollution as a matter of relationships among people, land, and water rather than a purely technical issue.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="Inclusive team composition">

Our seven subteams include members from molecular biology, computational biology, machine learning, hardware engineering, environmental science, and business. New members join through an open application process with no prior lab experience required because we found that experience requirements often exclude the students who most need the opportunity.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="Inclusive lab culture">

All members review a code of conduct at the start of the season. We train new members and we structure subteam work so first‑year students have the opportunity to present alongside senior members.

</Dropdown>

## Our Mission

### The Fragmented Crisis

It is hard to believe a solution to any problem can be achieved when its solvers are fragmented. Scale this to a crisis that affects every continent on Earth, and the ability to act efficiently and ethically can seem impossible. Plastic pollution is one such crisis; from the deep oceans to the human placenta, this pressing issue has proven to be immensely complex.

As a result, multidisciplinary action united against this threat is essential for any sustainable solution. However, this endeavor is remarkably difficult because the global plastics community is deeply disjointed, which heavily reduces efficiency in creating practical interventions.

### The Two Pillars of PetaBite

Human Practices presented a unique opportunity to bring together all sectors of the plastics community. To make the optimal, lasting difference, we realized a true solution must stand on two equal pillars:

1. **A robust, scalable technical solution** - leveraging engineering to solve the physical presence of plastic pollution around the world.
2. **A unified community platform** that bridges the disjointed plastics landscape by uniting data scientists, policy-makers, plant operators, ethicists, and local communities.

### The Iterative Feedback Loop

Our work this year used global stakeholder engagement to analyze the real-world challenges our project would encounter in unique contexts around the world. This created an iterative process that directly shaped our technical solutions and inspired us to build multidisciplinary frameworks to address the concerns raised in our interviews.

Using the core themes that emerged from these stakeholder sessions, our work bringing together the global plastics community culminated in the <strong>MERGE</strong> framework:

## Multidisciplinary Global Stakeholder Outreach

### Executive Overview

When we began the journey of seeking stakeholders, we knew we couldn’t only feature people who have historically had a seat at the table. We identified communities that are most affected by plastics, including Nigeria, Pakistan, Indonesia, and the Indigenous communities of Canada, to understand how our solution could fit into the unique environments and realities of these locations. This is because PetaBite's goal was always to follow the 2 pillars we identified for the best solution. Not only a great technical solution, but one that was built around the entire plastics community.

We organized our interviews into two streams, each designed to address a distinct category of questions.

### Industry and Community Interviews

These conversations tested both the feasibility and the ethics of deploying our technology beyond the laboratory. We spoke with wastewater treatment plant operators and environmental scientists about operational viability, with bioethicists and regulatory specialists about the governance structures a biological deployment would encounter, and with Indigenous scholars about environmental stewardship and our responsibilities to the communities and ecosystems affected by our work.

Several of these interviews directly informed our <strong>Regulatory Framework</strong> and <strong>Sustainability Report</strong>.

## Education and Multi-Directional Feedback

Our Human Practices Education arm explored how synthetic biology can be both communicated to and shaped by the communities it affects through open dialogue. While plastic pollution is a universally recognized crisis, enzymatic systems to degrade microplastics at the source remain unfamiliar to most communities. We set out to close that gap by making every activity this season a two-way exchange, where we learned as much as we taught. What we discovered about ethics, regulation, and the realities of industrial deployment ultimately changed how we developed PetaBite.

<Dropdown spotlight title="SPOTLIGHT: The UofT Microplastic Conference (September 19, 2026)">

### Please Visit our Conference Website! https://www.uoftmicroplastics.ca/

On <strong>September 19</strong>, iGEM Toronto hosted the <strong>UofT Microplastics Conference</strong> in close collaboration with the U of T Trash Team. The free, one-day conference took place at the Bahen Centre for Information Technology on the University of Toronto St. George campus. Under the theme <strong>"Rethink plastic,"</strong> we brought together researchers, students, and community members who work on or care about microplastics, and we built the day around two questions: <strong>what happens to plastic once it enters the environment and what we can realistically do about it.</strong>

### Overview

We built the conference to bridge the gap between research, public awareness, and action on plastic pollution. Microplastics research is moving quickly, and the evidence is growing faster than our shared response to it. Most of that research also stays inside academic journals and specialist meetings that students and the public rarely attend. Progress depends on researchers, the public and decision-makers working on this problem together, so we designed the conference to put all three in the same room.

We centred the day on freshwater microplastics, an urgent and under-monitored problem with direct relevance to Toronto. The Great Lakes hold 84% of North America's surface freshwater and roughly 20% of the world's, and researchers have found microplastics in every one of them. The problem reaches human health as well. Researchers have detected microplastics in human blood, placental tissue, and gastrointestinal samples, and current evidence points to exposure through both ingestion and inhalation. How that exposure happens, and what it means for health, remains open, and we wanted attendees to engage with those questions directly.

Our theme framed plastic pollution as a linear system, where plastic is made, used, and discarded, and asked attendees to consider what a circular one could look like. Answering that question requires chemistry, ecology, toxicology, policy, waste management and community behaviour, and no single field or technology covers it. We therefore drew speakers from ocean science, freshwater ecology, environmental chemistry, policy advocacy and community outreach, so attendees could see how these fields connect and where their own interests fit.

We also removed the barriers that keep people out of conferences like this one. Admission was free and open to anyone, and our call for abstracts invited undergraduate and graduate students from every discipline to present. We wanted students with no prior conference experience to have a genuine chance to present, and members of the public with no research background to feel welcome in the conversation.

Our partnership with the U of T Trash Team shaped this approach from the start. The Trash Team is a science-based community outreach organization of students, postdocs, researchers, local volunteers, and staff who work to increase waste literacy and reduce plastic pollution in local ecosystems. In May, both teams ran a series of cleanups across the Greater Toronto Area to prepare for the conference. During those cleanups, Trash Team members asked whether technologies like enzymatic degradation risk distracting from reducing plastic use. That question stayed with us through planning. It is why we built the program around prevention, policy and community action alongside technical solutions, and why we presented PetaBite as one approach among many.

### Statistics from the Event

Over 200 people registered for the UofT Microplastics Conference. Attendees included undergraduate and graduate students, faculty, researchers, environmental advocates, and members of the public, many of whom were attending a research conference for the first time.

The day ran from 8:30 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. and included a keynote address, <strong>five invited talks, our own project presentation, a community solutions talk from the U of T Trash Team, and a policy panel.</strong> Over lunch, <strong>15</strong> scientific posters were presented and judged in our Student Poster Session. Presenters came from Toronto Metropolitan University, Trent University, the University of Toronto and York University, along with researchers from the Rochman Lab, which gave attendees a view of plastics research happening across Ontario. For many student presenters, it was one of their first opportunities to share their work outside their own lab, and they did so directly with leading researchers and community members.

### The Program

#### Keynote: Microplastic Pollution

Dr. Kara Lavender Law, Research Professor of Oceanography at the Sea Education Association, opened the day. Dr. Law studies the sources, distribution, transformation, and fate of plastic debris in the marine environment. As a physical oceanographer, she has spent more than 12 months at sea aboard research vessels in the North Pacific and North Atlantic, sampling the regions where plastic debris accumulates in what are commonly called garbage patches. Her recent work moves upstream to how plastic waste is generated, transported, and treated, with the aim of stopping plastic from reaching the ocean in the first place.

Her keynote set the scale of the problem for the rest of the day and established the framing we returned to throughout: that the leverage lies in the systems producing and handling plastic, rather than in the ocean where it ends up.

#### Effect of Microplastics on the Environment

Dr. Chelsea Rochman, Associate Professor of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at the University of Toronto, spoke next. An internationally recognized leader in microplastic pollution research, Dr. Rochman develops monitoring methods and risk assessment frameworks that clarify the sources, fate, and impacts of plastic pollution in aquatic ecosystems. She also works with government and industry partners to translate that science into policy at the local, national, and international levels.

Her talk connected the keynote's global picture to the freshwater systems around Toronto, and her emphasis on what current monitoring methods can and cannot measure shaped how we think about the evidence a technology like PetaBite would need before deployment.

### STEAM-D from University of Toronto's Faculty of Medicine

The second team came through **STEAM-Design (STEAM-D), a program run by the University of Toronto Temerty Faculty of Medicine.** STEAM-D brings high school students who face systemic barriers to higher education onto the St. George campus for one month each summer. Students attend health science sessions led by researchers, graduate students and practitioners, then compete in a design competition where top teams win prizes and mentorship.

Our Co-President, Anthony Dinglasan, co-mentored a cohort of six high school students. He taught them the fundamentals of synthetic biology and guided them through the design challenge, and their project, PhosFlow, placed **first** out of 16 teams.

**What the team built:** PhosFlow addresses a problem in Canadian canola farming. Roughly 80% of Prairie soils bind phosphorus into insoluble forms that plants cannot absorb, so farmers spend over $1 billion each year on phosphate fertilizer to compensate. Much of that fertilizer washes into freshwater during snowmelt and rainfall, where it drives eutrophication and toxic algae blooms, and it exposes agricultural workers and nearby residents to chemical burns and respiratory harm. The team proposed an alkaline phosphatase spray that releases the phosphorus already present in the soil, which reduces how much new fertilizer a farm needs to apply.

Over four weeks, the students worked through the full arc of a mini iGEM synthetic biology project:

- **Enzyme production.** They proposed expressing alkaline phosphatase in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, then screening expressed variants through yeast assays to select the best performers.
- **Formulation.** They designed a stabilization strategy using cryoprotectants, phosphate and pH buffers, and microencapsulation, specifically to address why existing live-microbe bio-inoculants fail in Prairie winters and summer heat.
- **Competitive analysis.** They evaluated existing products, including Jumpstart and XiteBio Tuber+, and identified that both rely on live organisms that struggle in extreme conditions and work as additives rather than replacements.
- **Limitations.** They documented where their own approach would underperform, including drought conditions, high temperatures, dense grass systems and heavy metal contamination, and they proposed mitigations such as pre-planting application and root-zone targeting.
- **Regulatory pathway.** They determined that alkaline phosphatase is absent from Canada's Domestic Substances List, which requires a New Substances Notification, and that PhosFlow would register with the Canadian Food Inspection Agency as a supplement rather than a fertilizer.

## Regulatory Oversight

Throughout our stakeholder interviews, one recurring theme was the absence of regulatory frameworks capable of ensuring the safe, ethical, and responsible deployment of technologies like ours across many regions of the world. In response, we developed a comprehensive regulatory framework to evaluate how PetaBite could be governed throughout its lifecycle and across diverse jurisdictions. By identifying and synthesizing common principles shared among international regulatory bodies, we created a report that anticipates regulatory requirements and provides guidance for communities where formal oversight mechanisms may not yet exist.

Our framework examines the regulatory considerations relevant to PetaBite across open science, genetic resource access and benefit-sharing, dual-use research oversight, artificial intelligence governance, and plastics regulation. It also incorporates Canada's designation of plastic manufactured items as toxic under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act (CEPA), as well as the Federal Court of Appeal decision affirming that designation.

To support responsible global deployment, the framework profiles the regulatory landscapes of Pakistan, India, Nigeria, China, and Indonesia, identifying key governance considerations within each jurisdiction. It also establishes the ethical commitments that guide our work, including standards for data provenance, equitable access and deployment, and safeguards against waste colonialism, ethics dumping, and other forms of regulatory exploitation.

## Global Consolidated Database Interviews:

One of the greatest barriers to uniting the global plastics biodegradation community is the lack of a centralized, accessible resource that consolidates decades of research on plastic-degrading enzymes. To address this challenge, we developed the PETadex, a comprehensive database and enzyme atlas containing over one billion plastic-degrading enzyme candidate sequences derived from scientific research conducted around the world.

Designed as a resource built by and for the global scientific community, the PETadex transforms fragmented datasets and historical research into a unified, accessible platform. By bringing this knowledge together in a single location, the database enables researchers to more efficiently identify promising enzyme candidates, build upon existing discoveries, and accelerate innovation in plastic biodegradation.

To ensure the platform's long-term utility and sustainability, we conducted follow-up interviews with active researchers and domain experts. Their feedback directly informed iterative improvements to the database's user interface, functionality, and feature set, helping ensure that PETadex remains a practical and evolving resource for the broader scientific community.

#### Contents of interview:

Software and 3D printing iterate quickly and can be deferred, while long-lead items such as glassware, stir bars and custom caps need ordering early. On components, he recommended Adafruit’s StemmaQT connector standard over bare I2C wiring, since the software already exists and the parts cost less, and he shared his working performance targets: heating from ambient to 40 C, pumping at millilitres per minute or lower, mixing between 100 and 1,000 rpm, and optical density readings where higher opacity is tolerable. He explained the one-to-one reactor-to-Pi ratio as a deliberate choice for modularity, since a unit can be added or removed at will, with the Pi earning its place through the web server, plugin system and file system. On mechanical choices, he described screws as a historical accident that turned out well, since users understand them, they do not crack, they cost almost nothing, and they accept heat-set inserts. On failure modes he ranked glassware first as a consumable, followed by lost stir bars and failing vial caps, and on the electronics side pumps first, since motors wear out, then tubing, then integrated circuits. He preferred software interfaces over physical controls, since web UIs are cheap and fast to develop while physical interfaces add a development layer, though he noted a new Pioreactor with a screen is in progress. On running 96 units in parallel, he expected power consumption to matter less than we feared, with savings in the milliwatt range not worth chasing, and he flagged network latency as the likelier constraint if we transfer data frequently. He advised establishing a power budget in the range of 60 to 300 W, and confirmed that PCB heating is the cheapest option while cautioning that it heats from the side without liquid contact and will not be uniform.

#### How the Interview Shaped Our Project:

Cameron’s point that fractions of a percent in power efficiency are not worth redesigning around stopped us from optimizing a part of the system that does not constrain us, and his power budget range gave the hardware subteam a concrete figure to design against instead of an estimate. His warning about custom PCB development time moved us toward off-the-shelf boards and the StemmaQT connector standard, which removes both design time and driver work from our critical path. His ranking of failure modes gave us a maintenance and consumables plan we did not have, with glassware and pumps identified as the parts that need replacement rather than repair. His confirmation that a Pi Pico can replace the full Raspberry Pi without substantial changes, and that the Pi, STM and Arduino options perform comparably for our task, let us treat the controller choice as a cost and pin-count question. His note on long-lead components also reshaped our procurement timeline, since glassware and custom caps now sit at the front of our ordering schedule.

## Environmental and Sustainability Considerations

Our stakeholder interviews provided critical insight into the communities most affected by the plastic pollution crisis we seek to address. Because plastic pollution is a global challenge, creating a solution that can be deployed responsibly and equitably across diverse regions requires careful consideration of sustainability. No two communities face identical circumstances, and a solution that succeeds in one context may prove ineffective or even harmful in another.

Through these discussions, we identified three key areas that would shape the long-term sustainability of our project:

1. **Mitigating Operational Footprints:** Recognizing that training and running AI models, synthesizing enzymes, and operating laboratory and computational infrastructure require significant energy, water, and material resources.
2. **Preventing Secondary Pollution:** Assessing downstream impacts such as microplastic fate, biological and chemical waste management, and robust enzyme containment measures to minimize unintended environmental consequences.
3. **Advancing Circular Integration and Equity:** Ensuring that our solution can be integrated into existing systems without creating disproportionate burdens for vulnerable communities, while actively addressing concerns related to waste colonialism and unequal access to environmental technologies.

To evaluate and address these challenges, we developed a comprehensive sustainability report that critically assessed our project's environmental and social footprint. Guided by the concerns and perspectives shared by stakeholders, the report informed both the design and deployment strategy of PetaBite. This process helped ensure that our solution remained not only technically effective, but also socially responsible and equitable across diverse contexts.

## Final Thoughts

**Human Practices** served as the foundation of our project, guiding every technical subteam throughout the season. We approached plastic pollution as a global challenge that transcends geographic, political, and economic boundaries, recognizing that solutions developed in isolation rarely succeed at scale. To guide our work, we established two core pillars: **technical excellence** and **community co-creation.**

Stakeholder engagement directly influenced our project's direction. Conversations with plant operators, bioprocess engineers, researchers, and bioinformatics experts shaped key design considerations, including enzyme stability, microbial compatibility, degradation byproducts, ecological risk, and deployment feasibility. Their feedback also strengthened PETadex through improved evidence labeling, source traceability, and search functionality, while discussions around enzyme immobilization and washout reinforced our commitment to contained deployment.

These insights culminated in the **MERGE Framework,** our Human Practices roadmap for translating stakeholder perspectives into actionable design decisions. Built from approximately **70 stakeholder interviews, 3 conferences, 4 international exchanges, mentorship of 2 high school iGEM teams, outreach to over 800 students through 8 campus collaborations,** and extensive regulatory, sustainability, and database development efforts, MERGE provides a structured approach for integrating community engagement with technical development.

Biotechnology is filled with innovations that succeeded in the lab but failed in practice because stakeholder needs were considered too late. Through Human Practices, we brought those conversations to the forefront from the beginning. The commitments that emerged, including contained deployment, transparent science with clearly defined proprietary boundaries, and compatibility with existing infrastructure, now form a core part of PetaScale's industrialization strategy.

**Human Practices shaped what we built this season and defines what we are prepared to build next.**
