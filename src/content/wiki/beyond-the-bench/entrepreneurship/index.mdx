## Executive Summary

PetaBite and PetaScale embody a single, ambitious vision: transforming microplastic biodegradation from a student‑led initiative into a commercially viable, globally scalable platform. PetaBite, our iGEM cycle, served as the technical proving ground where a 66‑member team built and validated the scientific foundation through high‑throughput PETase discovery, enzyme engineering, and environmental testing.
While iGEM Toronto’s technical teams drove the experimental validation, the venture team refined product‑market fit, customer discovery, and regulatory mapping. Across eight national competitions, two global competitions, three international conferences, and eleven corporate sponsors, our business model was tested, refined, and validated.
Members of our team earned selection for YCombinator Summer School, showcased at WebSummit Vancouver, and presented at SynBioBeta Global: with each milestone underscoring the credibility and momentum of our work. Early interest from three venture capital firms and multiple startup accelerators further validated the commercial potential and urgency of our approach.
PetaScale now advances that foundation into full commercialization, translating proven science and market insight into real‑world deployment. Where PetaBite demonstrated feasibility and the open-source foundation of our project, PetaScale will translate that research into commercialization opportunities: building industrial partnerships, expanding infrastructure, and positioning itself as the leader in global biodegradation technology.

## Problem Statement

Plastic production has grown far beyond what global waste systems were built to handle. Annual output has surged from roughly 1.5 million tonnes in 1950 to more than 430 million tonnes in 2024, an increase of nearly 290-fold (PlasticsEurope, 2025). The curve continues to steepen. Under the OECD's baseline projections, global plastics use is projected to nearly triple to 1,231 million tonnes (1.23 billion) by 2060 in the absence of bold new policies, a faster rise than most raw materials (OECD, 2022). Over that same period, annual leakage into the environment is projected to double to 44 million tonnes (from about 22 million tonnes in 2019), while the stock of plastics accumulating in aquatic ecosystems is expected to more than triple, from roughly 140 million tonnes in 2019 to nearly 500 million tonnes in 2060 (OECD, 2022). Much of this material fragments into microplastics, particles smaller than 5 mm, that persist rather than biodegrade, infiltrating soil, waterways, food, and drinking water.
The human health evidence is now impossible to ignore. Micro- and nanoplastics have been detected in blood (Leslie et al., 2022), placenta (Ragusa et al., 2021), and organs including the kidney, liver, and brain, with concentrations in the brain rising by roughly 50% between 2016 and 2024 and peaking in patients with dementia (Nihart et al., 2025). Cardiovascular studies show similarly alarming results: the risk of heart attack, stroke, or death was 4.5 times higher among patients whose arterial plaque contained microplastics compared with those without (Marfella et al., 2024).
The contamination extends into agriculture. Microplastics accumulating in soil can disrupt plant metabolism, reduce yields, and harm the biodiversity of soil organisms (Zhou et al., 2021). Beyond surface contamination, there is growing evidence that the smallest particles are taken up by plants directly: micro- and especially nanoplastics can be absorbed through the roots and translocated upward via the plant's vascular system, accumulating in stems, leaves, and in some cases edible tissues and fruits (Li et al., 2020). This creates a route for plastic to move up the food chain, since animals, and people, that eat those plant parts ingest the particles held within them. Plastics in soil also act as carriers for pathogens. When plastic surfaces exposed to wastewater or livestock waste are colonized by microbial biofilms, this so-called plastisphere can harbor human pathogens and antibiotic-resistance genes (Zhu et al., 2022), and because microplastics cling to crops in ways that washing does not fully remove, they offer a direct vector for transferring disease onto food (Quilliam et al., 2023). The result is a compound threat to food security and ecosystem health.
Several initiatives have attempted to address plastic waste, membrane filtering, mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, and incineration among them, but none tackle the root cause. These approaches remain economically unfeasible, pose additional risks to human and environmental health, and even when scaled, fail to close the loop. Chemical recycling, for instance, can decompose PET into its monomers (TPA and EG) and reprocess them into resin, yet that resin is far more expensive than fossil-derived alternatives, rendering the process commercially unsustainable. The world needs a solution that eliminates plastic at its molecular source rather than repurposing or relocating it.
Regulators across major markets are converging on microplastics as a compliance priority, reframing remediation from a discretionary sustainability initiative into a legal obligation. The EU's 2023/2055 Regulation restricts synthetic polymer microparticles (European Commission, 2023), and the 2024/3019 Directive mandates microplastic monitoring in wastewater systems (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024). Canada's 2024 Federal Plastics Registry now requires detailed reporting from manufacturers and importers (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2024). More than 170 countries are negotiating a global treaty to end plastic pollution, signaling that remediation is shifting from voluntary sustainability to legal compliance (United Nations Environment Programme, 2022).
The timing for PETase innovation could not be more urgent. Regulation, science, and investment are finally moving in the same direction. Governments are tightening compliance frameworks, investors are backing enzyme engineering and advanced materials, and industries are actively searching for scalable biological solutions to a problem that has persisted for decades. Enzymatic degradation has evolved from a research concept to a commercial reality, with breakthroughs in engineered enzymes and metagenomic discovery driving the transition from containment to elimination. The scale of the challenge, its human and agricultural costs, and the accelerating regulatory landscape together define a market where technical advantage is both valuable and defensible, and PetaBite/PetaScale is built to capitalize on this opportunity by delivering the first truly scalable biological solution to global plastic pollution.
