## Problem Statement HAVE TO DO CITATIONS LATER

Global plastic production has grown far beyond what waste systems were built to handle. Annual output rose from about 1.5 million tonnes in 1950 to more than 430 million tonnes in 2024, a nearly 290-fold increase (PlasticsEurope, 2025), and the trajectory shows no sign of reversing. Under the OECD's baseline scenario, global plastics use is projected to grow from 460 million tonnes in 2019 to 1,231 million tonnes by 2060, outpacing most raw materials (OECD, 2022). Over the same period, annual plastic leakage into the environment is projected to double from about 22 million tonnes to 44 million tonnes, and the stock of plastic in aquatic ecosystems is projected to more than triple, from about 140 million tonnes to nearly 500 million tonnes (OECD, 2022). Much of this plastic breaks down into microplastics, particles smaller than 5 mm that persist for centuries under environmental conditions and spread through soil, waterways, food and drinking water.

### Effects on Human Health

Unfortunately, evidence of microplastics in the human body continues to grow. Researchers have detected micro- and nanoplastics in human blood (Leslie et al., 2022), placenta (Ragusa et al., 2021), and organs including the kidney, liver, and brain. Concentrations in brain tissue rose by about 50% between 2016 and 2024 and were highest in patients with dementia (Nihart et al., 2025). Cardiovascular data raise similar concerns. Patients whose carotid artery plaque contained microplastics had about 4.5 times the risk of heart attack, stroke or death over the following three years compared with patients whose plaque did not (Marfella et al., 2024).

### Effects on Agriculture

Microplastics also threaten agriculture. Microplastics that build up in soil can disrupt plant metabolism, reduce crop yields, and harm soil organisms (Zhou et al., 2021). The smallest particles can also enter plants directly. Crop plants absorb submicrometre plastics through their roots and move them into stems and leaves, including edible tissue such as lettuce leaves (Li et al., 2020). This creates a potential pathway for plastic particles to enter the food chain through the consumption of contaminated crops. Plastics in soil can also carry pathogens. When plastic surfaces come into contact with wastewater or livestock waste, microbial biofilms colonize them, and this community, known as the plastisphere, can harbour human pathogens and antibiotic resistance genes (Zhu et al., 2022). Because microplastics stick to crop surfaces and washing does not fully remove them, they may carry these pathogens onto food (Quilliam et al., 2023).

### The Status Quo

Current approaches to plastic waste include membrane filtration, mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, and incineration with each having major limits. Filtration captures microplastics and moves them into sludge or waste streams without breaking them down. Mechanical recycling degrades material quality with each cycle. Incineration releases emissions and destroys the material. Chemical recycling can break PET into its monomers, terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG), and rebuild them into resin, but that resin costs more than fossil-derived plastic, which limits its commercial viability. None of these approaches removes microplastics already in the environment at a cost that works at scale. The world needs a way to break plastic down into its molecular building blocks at the points where it accumulates.

### Regulatory Pressure

As a result, regulators across major markets now treat microplastics as a compliance issue. The EU Regulation 2023/2055 restricts the use of intentionally added synthetic polymer microparticles (European Commission, 2023), while Directive 2024/3019 requires member states to monitor microplastics in urban wastewater treatment systems (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024). In Canada, the Federal Plastics Registry mandates that manufacturers and importers report detailed data on the plastics they place on the market (Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2024). Since 2022, more than 170 countries have been negotiating a global treaty to end plastic pollution (United Nations Environment Programme, 2022).

### The Timing of PetaScale

Thus, the timing for PetaScale’s plastic degrading enzyme (PETase) technology is exceptionally strong. Governments are tightening compliance frameworks, and industries managing plastic waste urgently need scalable biological solutions to a problem that has persisted for decades. Advances in enzyme engineering and metagenomic discovery have brought enzymatic degradation closer to commercial viability. The scale of the challenge, its human and agricultural costs, coupled with mounting regulatory pressure, has created a market where technical advantage is both valuable and defensible. PetaBite/PetaScale meets this need through a scalable biological platform for PET microplastic removal, a solution explored in detail in the following sections.

## Solution

Plastic-degrading enzymes (PETases) are a promising solution to microplastic pollution and engineered variants such as FAST-PETase and LCC-ICCG already perform well under controlled lab conditions. However, two limitations still hold the field back: a lack of data and poor performance outside the lab. Most groups improve the same handful of known enzymes through rounds of engineering, which leaves most of nature's enzyme diversity untested. These enzymes also only work best under very narrow temperature, pH, and substrate concentration ranges. LCC-ICCG, one of the best-performing variants, needs temperatures near 70 °C, which makes it impractical in wastewater treatment plants, lakes and mixed waste streams. Wet lab testing is also slow, and while unlabeled sequence data is abundant, the field has very little labelled data on how enzymes perform across real operating conditions. Progress has therefore concentrated on optimizing a few known scaffolds for controlled reactor conditions, which leaves performance in the field largely unaddressed.

Our mission is to deploy the most efficient and versatile plastic-degrading enzymes at planetary scale. Our approach differs from existing work in two ways: we search nature's full enzyme diversity and we design and test every enzyme for the specific environmental conditions where it will be used.

We have already solved the first limitation: a lack of data. Before our work, researchers had characterized only about 495 plastic-degrading enzymes worldwide, far too few to train a model on which sequences degrade plastic and under what conditions. So our Dry Lab team built PETadex (petadex.net), the world's largest open-source database of plastic-degrading enzyme candidates, to close that gap. Using the Logan index, a planetary-scale metagenomic search system built from our associate lab, the RNALab identified about 300 million high-quality candidates from environments worldwide, expanding the searchable pool more than 600,000-fold. The first screen of 161 randomly selected candidates returned two that outperformed FAST-PETase, a leading engineered benchmark, which confirms that the database contains enzymes worth finding. Finally, we made the PETadex open access so any researcher can build on this diversity, since proprietary datasets and siloed efforts have slowed the field.

This iGEM cycle, we are solving the second limitation: performance outside the lab. Even the best PETases lose much of their activity in industrial conditions, at variable temperatures or alongside mixed contaminants. We are building a machine learning pipeline that uses the PETadex dataset to select and engineer enzymes for specific real-world conditions, starting with room-temperature activity. We test each candidate with high-throughput wet lab assays and an in-house bioreactor array that replicates the conditions enzymes face during deployment. Each round of testing feeds labelled data back into our models, so every cycle improves the next set of predictions. No other PETase effort we know of combines this scale of candidate discovery with systematic, condition-specific testing.

Our first target is wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), where our enzymes can degrade PET at ambient temperatures. We will then expand to landfill leachate and contaminated bioremediation sites, where PETases can break down accumulated PET in water and soil. We also see opportunities at the points where PET first enters the environment: degrading PET fragments shed during textile manufacturing, clearing PET microfibres from washing machine filters and dryer lint traps, and formulating PETases into detergents that degrade microfibres released from synthetic fabrics during the wash cycle.

## Value Proposition

Every company commercializing plastic degrading enzymes today builds around one engineered enzyme and one operating window. Companies such as Carbios, Samsara Eco, and Protein Evolution use enzymes to recycle concentrated, pre-sorted plastic inside controlled reactors, where they can heat the material, adjust pH and pretreat the plastic to suit the enzyme. Carbios, for example, grinds and amorphizes PET before degrading it near 70 °C. That model may work in a recycling plant, but it ultimately fails where microplastics actually accumulate. Heating the large volumes of water that flow through a wastewater treatment plant would be far too expensive, and conditions change from one part of a plant to the next: clarifiers run at ambient temperature, mesophilic anaerobic digesters run at about 35 °C, and thermophilic digesters run at about 55 °C.

PetaScale, on the other hand, matches the enzyme to the site. For each deployment point, we select and engineer an enzyme that works optimally at that point's existing temperature, pH and salinity, so the facility needs no heating, pretreatment or new infrastructure. This keeps retrofit costs low and lets one bioengineering platform serve multiple WWTPs, landfill leachates, facilities producing textile effluents, and appliance filtration environments.

Our competitive advantage is a growing portfolio of patented optimized enzymes, each engineered for a specific real-world condition. Engineered enzymes such as FAST-PETase and LCC-ICCG come from optimizing a small handful of known enzymes, which limits how far any design can extend beyond its original operating window. Our approach starts from a much larger set of natural sequences that evolved across a wide range of environments, some of which already compete with the best engineered enzymes, such as when the two top candidates from our first screen produced about four times more TPA than FAST-PETase under identical conditions. From these starting points, we engineer variants for each deployment condition and profile them on our bioreactor array across temperature, pH and salinity. Field data from every customer deployment then guides the next round of engineering. Although the PETadex may stay open to all researchers, the engineered enzymes we build on top of it are our own, patenting each one. Every new deployment site and every new vertical adds more protected enzymes to our portfolio, so our lead widens as we grow.

## Customer Segment Overview

PetaScale serves municipal and industrial organizations that need a scalable way to remove microplastics from their water and waste streams.
Beachhead market: wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs). WWTPs are our most technically validated and commercially promising entry point. Wastewater from homes and industry flows through these plants before it reaches lakes and rivers, which makes them the first point where operators can control microplastics before they spread downstream. We immobilize our PETases in packed-bed reactors, which degrade PET polymers inside the plant while keeping the enzyme contained. Through 20 interviews with operators, researchers, and technical staff across Ontario, Alberta, and Newfoundland, we confirmed that our approach fits existing plant workflows. Purchasing decisions in this segment involve municipal infrastructure directors, plant operators and environmental compliance officers. Regulatory pressure on these decision-makers is rising, led by the EU's new requirement to monitor microplastics in urban wastewater treatment (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024).

Beyond WWTPs, we have also explored expanding into three adjacent verticals.

**Textile effluent.** Textile manufacturers release PET fibres and fragments into their wastewater during production. PETases add an in-line treatment step that degrades this PET before the wastewater leaves the facility, with no change to the production process. Sustainability officers and production managers are the buyers in this segment and the treatment step supports their compliance and ESG reporting.

**Landfill leachate.** As plastic breaks down in landfills, it releases PET microplastics into leachate, which often flows to WWTPs or surface water. PETases can treat this leachate before it is discharged. Municipal waste authorities and environmental engineering firms that manage leachate treatment are the buyers in this segment.

**Filtration.** PETases built into filtration cartridges or post-wash modules can degrade microfibres where the filter captures them, before the filter is cleaned or thrown away. Customers include appliance manufacturers and water treatment technology providers that develop household, industrial and municipal filtration systems.

Noticeably, for filtration in particular, regulation is pushing this market forward. France passed the first law requiring microfibre filters in all new household and commercial washing machines from January 1, 2025 (Loi n° 2020-105, 2020, art. 79), although it should be noted that the government has not yet published the decree needed to enforce it (Pillefer, 2026). The EU is also developing standards to measure fibre shedding and filter efficiency and is reviewing its ecodesign rules for washing machines, with both processes expected to conclude by late 2026 (Krzan, 2025). In the United States, the proposed Fighting Fibers Act of 2025 would require a microfibre filtration system in every new washing machine sold (Fighting Fibers Act of 2025, 2025). As filters become standard, manufacturers will need a way to deal with the microfibres those filters collect. PetaScale plans to supply the enzymes that break down captured PET.

## Entrepreneurship Regulatory Landscape

Deploying an enzyme-based microplastic degradation technology in Canada requires navigating a two-tier regulatory system. Federal review determines whether the enzyme itself is legal to manufacture and use nationwide, while provincial review governs its deployment at specific provincial facilities.

This review will focus primarily on the regulatory landscape within Canada, where PetaScale plans to scale following iGEM. The regulatory report is most closely aligned with the WWTP vertical, reflecting its comparatively greater feasibility for near‑term deployment. Within Canada, operations within this vertical will focus on provincial regulations in British Columbia, Ontario, Alberta, and Newfoundland. These are the provinces where we have held promising initial feasibility discussions or identified the highest concentration of wastewater treatment facilities (WWTPs).

For additional insights into the bioethical considerations surrounding the industrial implementation of biological solutions, please refer to the Regulatory Report on the Human Practices page.

### Federal Regulatory Pathway: Canadian Environmental Protection Act (CEPA)

Before PetaScale's PETase enzyme can be manufactured or used in Canada, it must be assessed under CEPA's New Substances provisions, the federal framework governing whether any new chemical or biological substance is safe to introduce into Canadian commerce. This assessment is carried out jointly by Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC), the federal department responsible for environmental protection, and Health Canada, which evaluates potential human health impacts [1]. Together, these two departments determine whether a substance can legally be manufactured, imported, or used in Canada, and under what conditions they can be implemented.

PETase is classified for this purpose as a biochemical substance, which is the regulatory category applied to enzymes and other biologically-derived products where the substance itself (rather than the organism used to produce it) is what's being manufactured and deployed. This places PETases under the New Substances Notification Regulations (Chemicals and Polymers), commonly abbreviated NSNR, rather than the separate stream governing living organisms [2]. This distinction matters as it determines which specific dataset PetaScale must assemble and submit.

Two federal reference lists play a central role in scoping this dataset: the Domestic Substances List (DSL), which catalogues substances already recognized as manufactured or used in Canada, and the Non-Domestic Substances List (NDSL), its counterpart for substances already recognized in the United States [3]. A substance appearing on either list is treated as "existing" and is exempt from full notification; a substance appearing on neither is treated as new, triggering the complete notification process. As a computationally-designed enzyme sequence with no prior commercial precedent, PETase likely does not appear on either list and will accordingly be treated as a new substance requiring full notification.

The NSNR further distinguishes substances by manufacturing or import volume, assigning notification requirements under Schedule 10 for quantities between 1,000 and 10,000 kilograms per year, and Schedule 11 for quantities exceeding that threshold [4]. PETase does not qualify for the regulation's reduced-data pathway, which is reserved for polymers already listed or built entirely from already-listed components. Since neither condition applies to a novel enzyme sequence, PetaScale's notification is expected to proceed under Schedule 10 during pilot-scale deployment, transitioning to Schedule 11 as production scales toward commercial volumes.

The regulation imposes an additional, enzyme-specific requirement on top of this base schedule: where a biochemical substance possesses enzymatic activity, the notifier must also supply the information set out in Schedule 2, items 7 through 13: a dataset covering the enzyme's substrate specificity, optimal operating conditions, catalytic kinetics, cofactor requirements, and activity levels [5]. Since this data characterizes properties intrinsic to the enzyme itself, it will be generated through laboratory assays rather than compiled from existing literature that will constitute the most resource-intensive component of PetaScale's federal filing.

### Provincial Regulatory Pathway: Ontario Note: maybe dropdown will be nice for all the provincial ones

Once federal legality is established, deployment of PetaScale's enzyme technology within Ontario falls under the authority of the province's environmental ministry, the Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks (MECP), the body responsible for approving environmental activities and enforcing compliance across Ontario. MECP's authority to regulate wastewater treatment specifically flows from two statutes: the Environmental Protection Act (EPA), Ontario's broad environmental law governing discharges and contaminants generally, and the Ontario Water Resources Act (OWRA), a companion statute focused specifically on the protection and use of water resources, including sewage works [6][7]. Together, these two statutes give MECP the legal authority to require, review, and approve the specific kind of change PetaScale's technology represents.

Because PetaScale's enzyme is introduced as an additive into an existing, already-approved wastewater treatment facility, the relevant regulatory step is not a new application, but an amendment to that facility's existing Environmental Compliance Approval (ECA). An ECA is the core operating permit that MECP issues for a wastewater treatment plant, in which any facility introducing a new industrial process, which enzyme dosing constitutes, is required to amend its existing ECA to reflect that change [8].

An ECA amendment application of this kind requires a defined package of supporting technical documentation. At its core, this includes a process description explaining exactly how and where the enzyme is introduced: its dosing method, its point of entry within the treatment train, and how long it remains in the system before discharge, along with effluent discharge criteria showing what effect, if any, the enzyme has on the quality of the water leaving the plant.

This effluent data must be developed following MECP's own methodology for setting discharge limits, known as Procedure B-1-5, and must be paired with an assimilative capacity study confirming that the specific river, lake, or water body receiving the plant's discharge can absorb any resulting change in effluent characteristics without harm [11]. Finally, because a change of this nature can shift how a facility is categorized under MECP's own licensing system, the application must also include a facility reclassification assessment, confirming whether the plant's classification tier changes as a result of the new process.

Given the novelty of introducing a biological process additive that is opposed to the conventional chemical dosing MECP more routinely reviews, a pre-submission consultation with the ministry, held roughly nine months ahead of filing, is a reasonable step to take before formal submission [13]. Once filed, an ECA amendment of this kind can reasonably be expected to take over a year to review, though the actual timeline will depend on how the ministry scopes the assessment. Accordingly, PetaScale should reasonably adopt the full ECA pathway as its working planning assumption, subject to formal confirmation by MECP.

### Provincial Regulatory Pathway: British Columbia

Deployment of PetaScale's enzyme technology within British Columbia is regulated by the province's environmental ministry, the Ministry of Environment and Climate Change Strategy (ENV), the body responsible for issuing and enforcing environmental discharge approvals across BC. Within this ministry, oversight of wastewater and effluent discharge specifically falls to its Environmental Protection Division, the branch tasked with reviewing and administering discharge authorizations. This authority is established under BC's core environmental statute, the Environmental Management Act (EMA), and given further effect through two supporting regulations: the Waste Discharge Regulation, which sets out which activities and quantities of waste require formal authorization, and, specifically for municipal wastewater systems, the Municipal Wastewater Regulation, which establishes additional standards for municipally-operated treatment facilities [14][15]. Provincial approval in British Columbia determines whether and how PETase may be deployed at a specific wastewater treatment facility, independent of federal notification requirements under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act.

Under the EMA, discharging effluent requires an approved waste discharge authorization [14]. Since PetaScale's enzyme is introduced into a facility already operating under an existing authorization, the applicable mechanism is an amendment to that facility's permit, submitted to the ministry's Permit Administration office [16]. The EMA distinguishes minor amendments, covering modifications with an equal or lesser environmental impact, from major amendments, which involve a new contaminant type or altered discharge characteristics and require fuller technical review [17]. Because enzyme dosing introduces a biologically active substance not previously assessed under the facility's authorization, PetaScale's amendment is anticipated to be classified as major, subject to full technical review.

A major amendment of this kind must be supported by a defined set of technical documentation, structured by ENV around what it terms an Information Requirements Table (IRT) [18]. At its core, the submission must include a process flow diagram illustrating all waste streams and identifying the specific point at which the enzyme is introduced into the treatment process, alongside a full characterization of the resulting effluent: its source, quality, quantity, and frequency, along with the methodology used to generate that data. This must be accompanied by an environmental setting assessment of the receiving water body as it exists prior to the proposed change, conducted in accordance with the British Columbia Field Sampling Manual for Continuous Monitoring and the Collection of Air, Air-Emission, Water, Wastewater, Soil, Sediment, and Biological Samples [18]. Beyond this baseline characterization, ENV requires a Conceptual Site Model (CSM) mapping the contaminant's source, its transport pathways, and any potential receptors, distinguishing exposure pathways considered "complete" from those considered "incomplete" [19], as well as an assessment of the receiving water body's assimilative capacity, evaluating whether it can absorb the resulting change in discharge. Since PetaScale's effluent is discharged directly to surface water rather than through a subsurface system, this assessment is expected to also include an Initial Dilution Zone analysis, which quantifies the area around the discharge point within which the effluent is expected to dilute to ambient water quality standards [20].

Because enzyme-based treatment characterization falls outside the sampling protocols ENV has developed for conventional chemical effluent, direct consultation with ministry staff at the pre-application stage is expected to be necessary [18]. Unlike Ontario, where a nine-month pre-submission consultation window is formally recommended, British Columbia imposes no fixed scheduling requirement for this consultation.

British Columbia's pathway centers on amending the host facility's existing waste discharge authorization rather than pursuing a standalone application, with the amendment expected to be classified as major given the novelty of a biologically active additive. This classification triggers ENV's full Information Requirements Table, requiring PetaScale to generate effluent characterization, environmental setting, Conceptual Site Model, and assimilative capacity data, including an Initial Dilution Zone analysis given the facility's surface water discharge. As this data must be produced through laboratory and field assessment rather than compiled from existing sources, it represents the primary driver of both cost and timeline for PetaScale's British Columbia submission, and should be scoped directly with ENV once a specific host facility is confirmed.

### Provincial Regulatory Pathway: Alberta

Deployment of PetaScale's enzyme technology within Alberta falls under the authority of Alberta Environment and Protected Areas (AEPA), the provincial ministry responsible for environmental approvals across the province [21]. AEPA's authority to regulate wastewater treatment specifically derives from the Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act (EPEA), Alberta's core environmental statute, together with two supporting regulations that govern how the approval process actually works in practice: the Approvals and Registrations Procedure Regulation, which sets out the review process AEPA follows, and the Activities Designation Regulation, which specifies what level of authorization, registration or approval, a given activity requires [22].

This distinction matters directly to PetaScale's pathway: a registration applies to standardized, low-complexity activities governed by a pre-written Code of Practice, such as Alberta's codes for lagoon-based or collection-only systems, while an approval carries facility-specific terms for more complex operations like mechanical or activated-sludge treatment plants [21][23]. Since PetaScale's target host facilities are expected to be conventional municipal plants of this more complex kind, they would already operate under an individualized approval, meaning the relevant regulatory step is an amendment to that existing approval.

Alberta does not appear to organize its technical submission requirements around a single, standardized checklist. Instead, AEPA's review draws on its published Standards and Guidelines for Municipal Waterworks, Wastewater and Storm Drainage Systems, which sets baseline technical and effluent quality expectations for municipal systems [24], combined with the director's discretion to request any additional site-specific information considered relevant to the particular amendment under review [25]. In practice, this means the precise dataset AEPA will expect for an enzyme-dosing amendment is less fixed in advance than in Ontario or BC, and is more likely to be shaped directly through dialogue with the assigned application coordinator once a submission is underway.

Alberta's regulatory framework centres on director discretion rather than the published technical checklists used in Ontario and British Columbia, meaning PetaScale's submission requirements and review timeline cannot be finalized to the same degree of precision at this stage. PetaScale's planning therefore proceeds on two working assumptions: that its target host facility holds an individualized approval rather than a standardized lagoon registration, and that the proposed amendment will be treated as substantive given the biological nature of the additive. Early, direct engagement with AEPA's Regulatory Approvals Centre, once a specific Alberta host facility has been identified, will be essential to confirming these assumptions and establishing a defined submission pathway.

### Provincial Regulatory Pathway: Newfoundland and Labrador

Deployment of PetaScale's enzyme technology within Newfoundland and Labrador falls under the authority of the province's Department of Environment and Climate Change (ECC), and specifically its Water Resources Management Division, the branch responsible for regulating water and sewage systems across the province [26]. This authority derives from the Water Resources Act, the province's core statute governing the construction, operation, and modification of water and sewage works, which gives the department the power to issue and amend the permits these systems require [27].

Under the Water Resources Act, an existing sewage works, such as a municipal wastewater treatment plant, operates under a permit issued by the department, and any significant change to the design or operation of that works requires an amendment to the existing permit before the change can take effect, obtained specifically under Section 49 of the Act [27]. Because PetaScale's model introduces its enzyme into a facility already operating under such a permit, rather than establishing new treatment infrastructure, the applicable step is a permit amendment submitted on behalf of the host facility. Given that enzyme dosing constitutes a substantive change to how the works is designed and operated, introducing a biologically active additive not contemplated in the facility's original permit, this would be expected to qualify as a "significant change" triggering the Section 49 amendment requirement, rather than a change the department would treat as administrative.

Newfoundland and Labrador does not appear to organize its technical review around a standalone effluent-criteria procedure in the way Ontario's Procedure B-1-5 does or a structured information table in the way British Columbia's IRT does. Instead, effluent discharged from a permitted sewage works must comply with the province's Environmental Control Water and Sewage Regulations, made under the Water Resources Act, which set the baseline quality standards any discharge must meet [28]. Design and operational expectations more broadly are further shaped by the department's own published technical reference, the Guidelines for the Design, Construction and Operation of Water and Sewerage Systems, which sets expected engineering and performance benchmarks for these systems [26]. As with Alberta, the specific dataset the department will require to support an enzyme-dosing amendment is not laid out in a fixed public checklist, and would likely need to be scoped directly with the Water Resources Management Division once a specific host facility and process design are identified.

Since the province does not publish a standardized technical checklist or a stated review timeline to plan against in advance, PetaScale's working assumption is that enzyme dosing will be treated as a significant change requiring a Section 49 amendment, supported by effluent data demonstrating compliance with the Environmental Control Water and Sewage Regulations. This is a reasonable default given the biological nature of the additive, but as in Alberta, it should be confirmed through early direct engagement with the Water Resources Management Division once a specific Newfoundland host facility is identified.

## PetaScale Business Model

PetaScale is a B2B enzyme company. We engineer plastic-degrading enzymes to work under the conditions each customer already operates in.

Fortunately, published work shows this is achievable. Researchers have found PET-degrading enzymes in compost, marine, and soil environments (Tournier et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2022). Our PETadex database gives us 300 million natural candidates, so each engineered design begins from an enzyme that already works in conditions close to the customer's.

### How PetaScale Works:

1. **Defining the conditions.** The customer provides the operating conditions under which our enzymes must function: parameters such as temperature, pH, and salinity. These inputs define the design environment specifications for enzyme development.
2. **Engineering the enzyme.** We select the closest candidate from PETadex, engineer it to the specification, and validate its activity under the described customer's conditions.
3. **Building the delivery system.** We produce the enzyme and package it in the format its environment requires.
4. **Deploy.** The format depends on the vertical:
   - **WWTPs:** a packed-bed reactor holding immobilized enzymes, installed on a filtered side stream so it adds to existing infrastructure without interrupting plant flow.
   - **Textile effluent and landfill leachate:** metered enzyme dosing into existing treatment lines.
   - **Filtration:** enzyme loaded into cartridges and post-wash modules.

### How We Make Money:

- **WWTPs:** Utilities pay per million litres treated under multi-year contracts with regional authorities. The fee covers reactor installation, maintenance, replacement of spent enzyme cartridges, and delivery. Revenue recurs and grows with the volume of water treated.
- **Textile effluent and landfill leachate:** Operators pay for a pilot, which converts to a per-site service contract once we prove performance.
- **Filtration:** Manufacturers license the enzyme or buy enzyme-loaded cartridges as a component, and they keep control of their product and distribution.

### Why This Model Works:

We start with WWTPs because a small number of regional authorities control most of the treated water in a region, which gives us recurring revenue and field data early. Each new vertical reuses the same four-step pipeline under different conditions, so expansion costs far less than entering each market from scratch. Every deployment also returns activity data from real operating conditions, and that data shortens the next design cycle.

## Selling and Customer Relationships

The Business Model section sets out what our customers pay for. This section explains how we acquire those customers and retain them.

As aforementioned, our first customers run wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), which are regulated public infrastructure. An operator's first responsibility is keeping effluent within permit limits, and few operators will change a working process because a new technology sounds promising. For that reason, every sale starts with data from the customer's specified environmental conditions. We ask for a commitment only after the customer has seen our enzymes function within their respective use-case.

### How We Reach Decision-Makers:

Our sales process starts with feasibility conversations. Over the iGEM cycle, we spoke with 20 WWTP operators, engineers, researchers, and managers in Ontario, Alberta, and Newfoundland. Through these conversations we learned how plants run day to day, collected data on conditions that would affect enzyme performance, and who approves spending. The conversations also gave us our first working relationships with the staff who would run a pilot.

A single purchase at a municipal plant involves several people and each one needs different evidence. Operators and engineers want to know whether the enzyme will work in their plant, so we show them activity data measured under their own operating conditions. Infrastructure directors and compliance officers focus on risk. For them, we explain how a contained packed-bed reactor fits within existing permits and budgets, and how it prepares the plant for future microplastic monitoring requirements. Procurement officers manage the purchase itself. Public contracts usually go to competitive tender, so our pilot results help define the technical requirements for that tender.

Outside the municipal sector, our buyers include textile sustainability officers, landfill engineers and filtration product managers. These buyers weigh cost against compliance, so we begin with a contained, closed-loop trial on their own waste stream.

### From Feasibility Conversation to Contract:

A feasibility conversation converts into a contract in four steps:

1. **Sample collection.** We ask the customer for a sample of their material, such as thickened sludge, along with its temperature, pH and salinity. A customer who sends a sample and assigns staff time to the project has made a real commitment, and we treat that as the first sign of fit.
2. **Laboratory testing.** Before testing begins, the customer sets the success criteria. We then test our enzymes on the sample under the customer's conditions and measure PET degradation using methods both parties have agreed on.
3. **Results report.** We report how much PET the enzyme degraded, how quickly and at what dose. If the results meet the agreed criteria, the customer has evidence drawn from their own material.
4. **Pilot and contract.** A successful test leads to a pilot agreement, under which we install a packed-bed reactor at one plant. If the pilot meets its targets, it converts to a multi-year contract. We write our WWTP contracts to cover every eligible plant a regional authority runs, so a single pilot can open several plants.

We have completed the feasibility stage in the WWTP vertical, and we will use the same process for textile effluent, landfill leachate and filtration.

### What Product-Market Fit Looks Like:

We judge fit by what customers commit, since positive feedback costs a customer nothing. In each vertical, we look for a specific commitment:

- **WWTPs:** A regional authority provides sludge samples, signs a pilot agreement with defined success criteria, and budgets for a full contract once the pilot meets those criteria.
- **Textile effluent:** A mill provides production wastewater samples and co-funds an in-line trial, and it names a sustainability or production lead who is accountable for the result.
- **Landfill leachate:** A waste authority or engineering firm provides leachate samples, shares its treatment data and commits a site to a contained pilot.
- **Filtration:** An appliance or filtration manufacturer signs a joint development agreement to test enzyme-loaded cartridges in its own product.

### How We Retain Customers:

After a contract is signed, we report PET degradation and system performance to each customer on a fixed schedule, using the testing methods agreed at the start. Regular reporting keeps results visible to the decision‑makers who approved the purchase which is very critical when contracts come up for renewal. Each deployment also feeds field data back into our engineering pipeline, enabling us to design higher‑performing enzymes for that customer and others within adjacent verticals.
