## Protocol Summary

The following protocol summary describes the workflow for both the Biosensor and Selection assays:

1. Prepare plates
2. Integrate copper sulphate into PET mesh
3. Transform E. coli (BL21(DE3)) with PD-V2 plasmid (Empty or containing LCC, Fast-PETase, IsPETase, M4-Q IsPETase, Dead-IsPETase, or Dry Lab PETase sequence) and AS plasmid (containing either msGFP2 or sul1)
4. Plate transformed E. coli onto control (no mesh/no mesh or sulfadiazine) and test (with mesh) plates
5. Identify colony fluorescence using transilluminator (Biosensor assay)/look for colonies (Selection assay)
6. Pick colonies selected for by the assay and perform colony PCR
7. Send the resulting amplicons for sequencing to identify successful PETase variants and their corresponding colonies for downstream testing

## Copper Toxicity

E. coli cells can have their growth impaired by sufficiently high copper concentrations. To test the lethal copper dose, we ran a series of experiments testing E. coli dilutions (10¹⁰, 10⁸, 10⁷, 10⁶ 10⁵,10⁴, 10³, 10²) on a range of copper concentrations (0mM, 0.25mM, 0.5mM, 1mM, 2mM, 4mM, and 8mM) mixed onto agar plates. For details, see the Copper Plate Testing document below. It was determined that the range of all plates up to 4mM (108 to 102) did not alter bacterial growth detrimentally. Finding the highest copper concentration that does not inhibit bacterial growth informs how much copper can be integrated into PET for the assay.

## Copper leakage and background fluorescence

We conducted a leakage test to ensure that the PET mesh won’t leach out copper. If the mesh released copper without requiring any PETase activity, this would result in false positives. Thus, we transformed our E. Coli cells with only the biosensor AS plasmid and placed the PET mesh on top of the agar to see if any cells would fluoresce without PETase activity. As seen below, copper was not detected in the cells, indicating that copper leakage from the PET mesh did not occur. Additionally, background fluorescence was insignificant.

## Copper-Induced GFP Expression

To ensure that our AS plasmid expresses msGFP2 in the presence of copper, we conducted a serial dilution experiment to determine which concentrations of copper colonies would fluoresce. To observe the fluorescence, a UV light was shone onto the colonies. In the images below, it can be seen that the reporting plasmid functions within a certain range. Plates were made using 38g/L MHA in MilliQ water. After microwaving, sterile CuSO₄ was added to make 0mM, 0.25mM, 0.5mM, 1mM, 2mM, 4mM, and 8mM plates. Kanamycin was kept at 50ug/mL. BL21(DE3) transformed with AS-msGFP2 was liquid cultured overnight. The cells were washed with saline and spotted onto the plate at different dilutions (dilutions deviate from protocol, see Figure 3).

## Testing Assay with Known PETase

Lastly, we wanted to conduct a positive control to see if the biosensor would perform as expected with a working PETase. Thus, we transformed our cells with a PD-V2 plasmid containing a known PETase (LCC, Fast-PETase, IsPETase, M4-Q IsPETase, Dead-IsPETase) and the reporting plasmid. Empty PD-V2 was also transformed and tested as a control. We then placed the copper mesh onto our agar and let it grow overnight to see if the colonies would fluoresce. For the Biosensor assay, only the plate containing M4-Q IsPETase grew in our initial test. However, these colonies exhibited fluorescence, providing evidence that the Biosensor assay is functional (Figure 6).
